Holiday Weight Gain in Dogs: Weigh Yours Before Thanksgiving

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

On the Tuesday before Thanksgiving the clinic scale read 19.8 lb. On January 6 the same scale reads 21.6. Nothing happened on any one of those days that would have worried anybody, which is why 1.8 lb went by without a comment.

Two pounds is a rounding error on a Labrador and nine percent of the dog here. It is the gain veterinary weight programs see most often between late November and early January.

Quick answer: A pound of body fat is roughly 3,500 calories, and 36 days sit between Thanksgiving and New Year's Day. Spread a pound across them and a dog needs about 97 extra calories a day to end the year heavier. A full plate, around 1,100 calories for a 20 lb dog, is 0.31 lb of that. The rest arrives in the quiet weeks after, which is why the weigh-in worth doing happens this week rather than in January.

The Day Is Worth a Third of a Pound

A dog that eats the whole plate on Thursday takes in 0.31 lb worth of fat in about four minutes. That is the part people plan for. Gates go up, guests get warned, the pie moves to the back of the counter.

Then Friday arrives and the rules go soft. Leftovers sit in the refrigerator for four more days, somebody brings biscuits as a host gift, and the evening walk shortens as the light goes. Thirty-six days at 600 extra calories would be six pounds and no dog manages it. Thirty-six days at 150 is 1.5 lb, and that is an ordinary American December for a dog with a family that loves it.

What a Pound Costs

Pounds are what owners measure. Calories are what the body counts. The 3,500-to-one conversion is the one veterinary weight programs use, and it holds across breeds: a Chihuahua and a Great Dane pay the same price per pound.

That fixed price hides an asymmetry. Ninety-seven calories is a third of the day for a 10 lb dog and six percent for a 100 lb one, so the small dog gains a pound on a surplus the big dog absorbs without changing shape.

The Wednesday Weigh-In

The date that matters is not January 1. It is the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, when the dog is still at its usual weight and the plan can change instead of the outcome.

One scale, one time of day. Before breakfast, after the first trip outside, on the same hard floor. A clinic lobby scale is free, calibrated and reads to a tenth of a pound. A bathroom scale is plus or minus a pound, which on a 10 lb dog is a ten percent error and enough to hide the whole holiday.

Three things are worth buying before Thursday. A kitchen scale that reads in grams, so the kibble stops being a guess. A baby scale under 25 lb, since 0.2 lb increments are the only way a fifth of a pound becomes visible. And a treat jar with a lid.

Sit with the biscuit row. Two and a half medium biscuits a day for five weeks is a pound, and that is what a house with visitors produces without anyone deciding to feed the dog. The pie rows run the other way: eleven slices across 36 days, so one slice every three days does the same thing and still feels like a tradition.

The Other Half of the Ledger

Food is the half owners can see. The other half is everything that stops in November.

Daylight ends before 5 pm across most of the country by early December, turning a 45 minute evening walk into a 20 minute loop around the block. Losing 25 minutes costs 30 to 50 calories, so exercise explains the second biscuit and not the first.

Guests carry the rest. The host gift of biscuits, the kids under the table, the neighbor with a bag of jerky, the walker who hands over a treat after every route. Four people adding 40 calories a day is 160, and 160 for 36 days is 1.6 lb.

Name one gatekeeper for the week and let that person hold the jar. It is a boring rule that beats willpower, because it takes the decision away from whoever has already had two glasses.

The January Reckoning

Weigh the dog in the first week of January, same scale, and compare against the number on the food bin. Most of the time the answer is half a pound to two pounds, which on a small dog is 2 to 10 percent of body weight.

Body condition scoring is the cross-check. One point on the nine point scale is roughly 10 to 15 percent of ideal weight, so a dog that slid from a 5 to a 6 during December has spent the entire holiday gain on one increment. The Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator returns both.

Undoing it follows the 3,500 rule in reverse, with a speed limit. Veterinary weight loss targets 1 to 2 percent of body weight per week, so 1.8 lb on a 20 lb dog is five to nine weeks. The Thanksgiving pound is usually gone in March.

The mistake in January is the crash. Cutting a 512 calorie ration by 40 percent leaves 307, below the 366 the dog needs at complete rest, and a diet under resting energy requirement costs lean mass rather than fat.

One pattern belongs to the vet rather than the calendar. A dog gaining on an unchanged ration, or looking heavier while eating less, needs thyroid testing, ideally free T4 by equilibrium dialysis paired with TSH, plus a conversation about hyperadrenocorticism. Unexplained weight loss is more urgent than unexplained gain.

Holiday Weight Questions

How much weight can a dog gain between Thanksgiving and New Year?

Half a pound to two pounds for a dog under 25 lb, two to four for a large breed, and it tracks the surplus rather than the size of the animal. The 97 calories a day that adds a pound is fixed, so a small dog reaches it with one biscuit while a large one needs a biscuit and a roll with butter.

Is it cruel to skip the Thanksgiving plate?

No, and it is not necessary either. One plate is 0.31 lb, a third of the whole holiday gain, and a dog that gets nothing learns the counter is worth checking. Use the 10 percent rule: 51 calories of turkey for a 20 lb dog, in its own bowl, away from the table.

How long does it take to lose holiday weight?

At 1 to 2 percent of body weight per week, 1.8 lb on a 20 lb dog takes five to nine weeks. Faster costs muscle, and a dog that loses muscle comes back heavier the next time it is fed normally.

Two numbers decide this holiday and neither is on the table: the weight on Wednesday, and 97 calories, the daily surplus that turns five weeks of ordinary generosity into a pound.

Call the vet before January if the dog gains on a measured ration, drinks more than usual, or carries a belly that feels tight rather than soft. Call during the holiday if it stops eating, vomits repeatedly, or cannot hold water down. A holiday pound is a March problem. Those are a today problem.
