Shih Tzu Calorie Calculator: Daily Food Needs

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published November 4, 2026 · 8 min read

A twelve-pound Shih Tzu needs about 349 calories a day at ordinary house-dog activity. That works out to 97 grams of kibble, or 48 grams at each of two meals.

The harder question is whether your dog actually weighs twelve pounds. A full coat stands two inches off the body in every direction, which covers the ribs, the spine and the waist in one motion. The dog on your lap and the dog on the scale are two different animals. Only one of them eats.

The arithmetic: RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. A 12 lb (5.4 kg) Shih Tzu rests at 249 kcal. Multiply by 1.2 for a neutered lap dog (299), 1.4 for one that walks twice a day (349), or 1.6 for a dog covering real distance (399). A loss plan runs 1.0 to 1.2 times the resting number for the weight the dog should be. The Dog Calorie Calculator runs the same steps and returns cups and grams with the kcal.

The Band Runs From Nine to Sixteen Pounds

The American standard puts the Shih Tzu at 8 to 11 inches at the shoulder and 9 to 16 pounds, on a body slightly longer than tall. FCI standard No. 208 opens the top wider: 4.5 to 8.1 kg, roughly 10 to 18 pounds. Two registries, and the European ceiling sits two pounds higher.

The standard also calls for substantial bone and a sturdy build, so a heavy Shih Tzu reads as well made in a way an overweight Chihuahua never does. Seventeen pounds is not a disqualification. It is a body condition score of seven that no one has put a hand on.

Nine pounds to sixteen is 78 percent more dog. Because the 0.75 exponent flattens as weight climbs, the calorie difference is only 54 percent. One marginal pound costs about 15 calories a day at the 1.4 factor. That is invisible in a bowl and four percent of the day, every day.

The gram columns are the ones that change what lands in the bowl. A 16-pound Shih Tzu at 1.4 eats 120 grams a day. The nine-pound one eats 78. Same food, same scoop, 42 grams apart, and neither dog will tell you the difference.

The Coat Is a Blindfold

Put both thumbs on the spine just behind the shoulder blades and wrap your fingers around the rib cage. You want the ribs to feel like the bones on the back of your hand, under a thin layer of padding, not like your knuckles. Part the hair or slide your hand up under the coat from behind the elbow. On a coated breed this is the only check that works.

A wet Shih Tzu is a shock the first time. Two inches of hair come off in the tub and the dog underneath is either fine or plainly fat. Groomers see that shape every four to six weeks and read it better than the owner does. Ask yours to put the dog on the salon scale. That number costs nothing, and it beats a bathroom scale held with a dog in your arms, where a one-pound error is eight percent of the animal.

A harness that still buckles measures the chest, not the fat over the ribs.

Feed the Weight the Dog Should Be

The calculator takes one weight, and this is where most home feeding plans go wrong. If the dog is carrying extra, the number you enter has to be the weight it should be at a body condition score of five. Enter today's weight on an overweight dog and the surplus gets baked into the target, which is why a dog can gain on a portion that was measured to the gram.

On a nine-point scale, each point above five is roughly 10 to 15 percent over ideal. On a twelve-pound frame that is a little over a pound per point.

Start at 299 and drop toward 249 if the scale has not moved in three weeks. The Factor Is Smaller Than the Breed Write-Up Suggests

1.2 is a neutered dog that sleeps most of the day and gets a short walk. 1.4 assumes two walks totaling thirty to forty minutes. 1.6 means an hour plus real off-leash running, which very few Shih Tzus get on a weekday. Most of these dogs live at 1.2, and their owners feed 1.6.

Heat is the seasonal version of the same error. The breed is brachycephalic, mildly compared with a French Bulldog but enough that sustained trotting above eighty degrees is limited by the airway rather than by willingness. A July ration is honestly a 1.2. A January ration can be 1.4. That fifty-calorie gap is fourteen percent of the day, and almost nobody changes the scoop with the season.

Cold runs the other way here. The double coat insulates, so a Shih Tzu in full coat needs no winter calories. Add ten percent for one clipped short in a cold climate.

The Mouth Is What Quietly Moves the Number

The standard calls for an undershot bite on a short muzzle, and forty-two teeth have to fit a jaw shorter than the tooth row wants. Crowding at the incisors and canines, retained baby teeth, and periodontal disease arrive earlier in this breed than in a long-nosed one.

Watch the bowl. A dog that drops kibble, chews on one side, tilts its head while eating, or slows down over a week is telling you about its mouth rather than its appetite. So is breath that smells like a drain.

The usual fix makes the arithmetic worse. Kibble runs 3.5 to 4.0 kcal per gram. Canned food runs about 1.0 to 1.2. A three-ounce can is 90 to 100 calories, which is 26 to 29 percent of a 349-calorie day, added on top of a full ration. Soft food also feeds the plaque that caused the problem. Hold the calories steady and book the dental cleaning instead.

Weight Loss on a Full Bowl

Hereditary kidney disease is documented in this breed, and it tends to surface in a young adult rather than a senior. The failing tissue gives no sign until a lot of it is gone. Water is the first clue.

A Shih Tzu that starts drinking more, asks to go out at night again, and loses weight while eating normally or more needs bloodwork, not a bigger portion. BUN, creatinine, SDMA and a urine specific gravity answer the question in an afternoon. A renal diet is protein-restricted but calorie-dense, so the 349 target still holds. The food changes, not the number.

Three findings that mean a vet visit, not a feeding change: weight loss with increased drinking or urination at any age · dropping kibble or chewing on one side for more than a few days · noisy breathing or reluctance to keep walking once the temperature passes eighty. Each has a test attached, and none of them is solved by adjusting the scoop.
