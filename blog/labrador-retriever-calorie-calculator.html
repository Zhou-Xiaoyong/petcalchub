Labrador Retriever Calorie Calculator: Daily Food Needs

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 17, 2026 · 8 min read

The Labrador Retriever is the most common dog in the waiting room and, in most weight clinics, the most common overweight dog in the waiting room. Labs were bred to work all day in cold water, retrieve whatever fell and eat whenever food was available — a combination that made them superb gundogs and makes them easy to overfeed on a sofa. Getting the calorie number right means accepting that this breed will not self-regulate.

Quick answer: A 70 lb (32 kg) neutered adult Labrador needs roughly 1,500 kcal a day — about 4 cups of a typical 380 kcal/cup large-breed formula. Use RER = 70 × (kg)0.75, then multiply by 1.6 for a neutered adult, 1.0 for weight loss, 1.4 for a senior and 2.0-2.5 for a working gundog. Run your own dog's weight through the Dog Calorie Calculator.

Why a Labrador Needs Its Own Calculation

Breed matters because breed sets the weight range you feed to. The breed standard puts adult males at roughly 65-80 lb (29-36 kg) and females at 55-70 lb (25-32 kg). A 90 lb Labrador is not a big Labrador — it is an overweight Labrador, and feeding it as though 90 lb were healthy locks the excess in place.

That is the first correction most owners need. Calorie targets should be built on the dog's ideal weight, not its current weight, whenever the two differ. If your Lab carries an extra 10 lb, you are feeding the metabolism of a dog that does not exist. Check the ribs and waist against a body condition chart, or use the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator to estimate a realistic target before you calculate anything.

The Formula, Run on a Real Labrador

Veterinary calorie estimates start with resting energy requirement, the energy a dog burns at complete rest. It scales with body weight to the 0.75 power, not linearly, which is why a 70 lb dog does not simply eat twice what a 35 lb dog eats.

Take a 70 lb male Lab. Divide by 2.2 to get 31.8 kg. Raise 31.8 to the power 0.75, which gives about 13.4, and multiply by 70. RER lands near 940 kcal a day. Multiply by 1.6 for a neutered adult and the maintenance target is about 1,500 kcal. The same dog on a weight-loss plan uses a factor of 1.0 and a target weight of, say, 65 lb — roughly 890 kcal, a 40 percent cut that should produce about a pound of loss per week.

Per-kilogram needs fall as body size rises: at the 1.6 factor a 70 lb Lab needs about 47 kcal per kilogram, while a 15 lb Beagle needs closer to 62. Your Labrador eats less per pound than a much smaller dog.

The Labrador Appetite Gene

The breed's reputation for bottomless hunger is not owner exaggeration. In 2016 a group sequencing Labrador DNA identified a 14-base-pair deletion in the POMC gene, part of the hypothalamic appetite-signalling pathway. Roughly a quarter of Labradors carry at least one copy. Carriers scored markedly higher on food-motivation questionnaires, weighed on average about 2 kg more, and were described by owners as more likely to beg and scavenge.

The practical meaning is important. The gene does not appear to drop metabolic rate — carriers do not burn fewer calories. It appears to blunt the satiety signal, so a carrier empties the bowl and still feels hungry. That is why "feed until the dog seems full" is useless advice for this breed, and why a measured portion beats intuition. A calorie deficit still works in a carrier: the physiology of weight loss is unchanged, the dog simply complains more loudly.

Picking the Right Factor

Most Labrador households start at one of four numbers: 1.3-1.4 for a couch companion, 1.6 for a neutered pet, 1.8 intact, 1.4 for a senior over eight, and 2.0-3.0 for a Lab doing real retrieves or field trials. The working figure gets overused — two walks around the block is not light work in the working-dog sense, and most pet Labradors belong nearer 1.3. If your dog gains on the calculated amount, drop the factor 10 percent and re-weigh in three weeks.

From Calories to the Bowl

Calories are the decision; cups are the delivery. A typical large-breed kibble runs 340-400 kcal per cup, and our calculator uses 380, so a 1,500 kcal target is about 4 cups across two meals. Switch to a 450 kcal/cup performance formula and the same dog needs 3.3 cups. Owners who compare cup counts between foods without comparing calorie density are where most Labrador weight problems begin. Treats, Chews and the 10 Percent Rule

Treats should stay under 10 percent of daily calories. For a Lab on 1,500 kcal that is a 150 kcal budget — roughly three medium biscuits or one dental chew. Most owners spend it before 9 a.m. and then cut the kibble ration. On training days, weigh treats out of the bowl rather than adding them.

Two traps are specific to this breed. Labs are accomplished counter surfers, so unmeasured extras are common. And Labs are among the breeds at elevated risk of gastric dilatation-volvulus, so one large meal, vigorous exercise within an hour of eating and raised bowls are worth avoiding.

Checking the Number Against the Dog

Any calculated figure is a starting estimate, not a verdict. Weigh your Labrador monthly on the same scale, score body condition every few weeks, and adjust by 10 percent in the direction the scale demands. A healthy Lab has ribs you can feel under a thin fat layer, a visible waist from above and a tucked-up belly. If the ribs need pressing to find, the target is too high.

For weight loss, aim for 1-2 percent of body weight per week — about 0.75-1.5 lb for a 75 lb dog. Faster risks lean muscle loss and, in a breed prone to joint disease, makes little sense. If the scale has not moved in four weeks on a measured ration, the food is under-measured, the treats under-counted, or a medical issue such as hypothyroidism needs checking.

The Bottom Line

A Labrador's calorie need is a formula plus a judgement call. The formula gives roughly 1,250 kcal for a 55 lb female and 1,575 for a 75 lb neutered male. The judgement call is deciding whether your dog is a 1.3 couch companion or a 2.0 field dog, and being honest that most pets are the former. Add the breed's genetic hunger drive — the bowl gets measured and the dog does not get a vote — and you have a workable plan: calculate, measure, weigh monthly, and let the scale decide what happens next.
