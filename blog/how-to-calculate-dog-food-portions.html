How to Calculate Dog Food Portions by Calorie

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published September 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Almost every dog feeding problem traces back to the same missing step. The owner knows roughly how many calories the dog should eat, then reaches for a measuring cup. But a cup measures volume and kibble is sold by weight, so two scoops of two different foods can differ by hundreds of calories while looking identical in the bowl. The fix takes ninety seconds of arithmetic and one cheap kitchen scale.

Quick answer: Divide your dog's daily calorie target by the food's calorie density to get grams per day. If the label says 3,500 kcal ME/kg and your dog needs 700 kcal, that is 700 ÷ 3.5 = 200 g of food per day, split across meals. Use the Dog Calorie Calculator for the target, then read the density off the bag.

Why a "Cup" Is Not a Portion

Kibble is extruded at different densities. A cup of a light, airy formula may weigh 85 grams; a cup of a dense, small-kibble food can weigh 120 grams or more. Multiply that 40% spread across two meals a day for a year, and two owners following the same "one cup twice a day" instruction — with the same brand's scoop — can feed their dogs meaningfully different amounts of energy.

There is a second, quieter problem. Scoops are not standardized, and owners over-scoop more often than they under-scoop, with the error growing as the portion gets smaller. For a Chihuahua eating 200 kcal a day, an extra 30 grams is a 10% calorie increase, enough to drive slow weight gain nobody notices until the collar gets tight. The remedy is not a better scoop; it is switching from volume to mass.

Step 1: Get Your Dog's Daily Calorie Target

Everything downstream depends on this number, so start here rather than with the bag. A dog's daily energy target comes from the resting energy requirement — RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75 — multiplied by a life-stage and activity factor. A typical neutered adult uses about 1.6; puppies, working dogs, and intact animals need more, while seniors and dogs on a weight-loss plan need less.

The Dog Calorie Calculator returns the target in seconds, and the Breed Calorie Calculator adds breed-specific size tendencies. For an overweight dog, set the target from the ideal weight rather than the current one — feeding for the current weight simply maintains the problem — and use the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator to establish that reference weight.

Step 2: Read the Calorie Density Off the Label

Labels state energy content two ways, and you want the figure per kilogram. Look for a phrase like "3,540 kcal ME/kg" on the guaranteed analysis panel or beside it. ME means metabolizable energy — the calories the dog actually uses after digestion — and it is the correct number for portion math. A label may also print "kcal ME/cup", which is convenient but derived, because it assumes a particular weight per cup for that formula. If both figures appear, use kcal/kg and weigh the food. Watch for the rare label that lists gross energy (GE) instead of ME; GE is always higher and will cause you to underfeed.

Density varies a lot between products, and the difference shows up directly in how much food goes in the bowl:

Food type Typical ME density Grams for 700 kcal Approx. cups
Light / weight-control kibble3,200 kcal/kg219 g~2.1
Typical adult maintenance kibble3,500 kcal/kg200 g~1.9
High-energy / performance kibble3,800 kcal/kg184 g~1.8
Puppy / high-fat sport formula4,100 kcal/kg171 g~1.6
Typical wet food (cans)~1,000 kcal/kg700 g~2.5 cans

Read that as a warning about substitutions. Switching a dog from a 4,100 kcal/kg puppy formula to a 3,200 kcal/kg light food cuts the calories per gram by nearly a quarter, so the same measured volume delivers far less energy. The cup equivalents above assume kibble weighing about 105 g per 8 oz cup — check your own food, because that assumption is exactly what we are trying to eliminate.

Step 3: Convert Calories to Grams

The arithmetic is one division: calories per kilogram divided by 1,000 gives calories per gram, and the daily target divided by that gives grams per day. A 30 lb dog with a 700 kcal target eating food labelled 3,540 kcal ME/kg works out as 3.54 kcal per gram, so 700 ÷ 3.54 = 198 grams, rounded to 200. If the label only gives kcal per cup, divide instead — 700 ÷ 380 kcal/cup is 1.84 cups. Write both numbers down; the gram figure is the one you will actually use.

Then split the daily total across meals. Two meals suits most adult dogs and is easier on the stomach than one large one, which also lowers bloat risk in deep-chested breeds. Puppies under about four months do better on three or four smaller meals, because stomach capacity is limited relative to their very high energy needs. Our example is 100 g morning and night — and anything that goes into a puzzle feeder counts as part of the ration, not as a bonus.

Step 4: Weigh It, Don't Scoop It

This is the step that makes the others work. Put the bowl on a digital kitchen scale, press tare, and pour until the number reads your target. A basic one-gram scale costs less than a bag of premium treats and lasts for years.

Weigh for at least two weeks before trusting your eye. Most owners find their habitual scoop was off by 10 to 30%, and that correction alone resolves a surprising number of "my dog is gaining weight on a measured portion" complaints. After that, keep a pre-portioned container per meal, or make up a week of daily bags at once — both beat improvising at 6 a.m.

Mixing Wet Food, Dry Food, and Toppers

Wet food is roughly three times less calorie-dense than dry, which surprises owners who add a can on top of a full measured ration. Decide how many calories each component should supply and convert each separately. On a 700 kcal target split 60/40, 420 kcal of dry food at 3,540 kcal/kg is 118 g, and 280 kcal of wet food at 1,050 kcal/kg is about 267 g — close to one standard 13 oz can. Toppers work the same way: a tablespoon of plain cooked chicken is trivial, but a quarter cup of a calorie-dense topper every day belongs inside the budget, not beside it. Hydration matters too on a dry-heavy diet, so the Dog Water Intake Calculator is worth a look for a daily water target by weight.

Count the Treats Inside the Budget

The most common reason a perfectly calculated portion stops working is uncounted food. A widely used guideline is that treats and everything outside the main ration stay under 10% of daily calories, with balanced food making up the other 90% so the diet remains complete. On a 700 kcal plan that is 70 kcal — a couple of small biscuits, not a handful. If you train heavily, take it from the ration instead: weigh the day's food in the morning, set a portion aside for training, and feed the rest as meals.

When the Portion Stops Working

A calculated portion is a starting hypothesis, not a sentence. Individual metabolism varies by as much as 20% between dogs of the same size, so check the dog rather than the number every two to four weeks: run your hands along the ribs, look for a visible waist from above, feel the hip bones. The Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator turns those observations into a condition score if you want a second opinion.

If the dog is gaining on a measured portion, confirm the scale is accurate, check that every household member is using the same number, then cut calories by about 10% and re-weigh in two weeks. If the dog is losing weight it should not be losing, add 10% the same way. Two adjustments at 10% beat one drastic change, because dropping below RER is a health risk rather than a diet — and a dog that keeps losing weight on a correctly measured portion needs a veterinarian, not more food.

The Bottom Line

Calculating a portion by calorie is three steps: get a daily energy target from weight and life stage, read the food's kcal ME/kg off the label, and divide. Weigh the result in grams instead of scooping by volume, count treats inside the budget, and recheck body condition every few weeks. The arithmetic is simple — the reason it works is that it replaces three unreliable estimates, the scoop, the "about a cup", and the ignored treat, with one measured number you can actually adjust.
