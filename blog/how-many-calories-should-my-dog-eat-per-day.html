How Many Calories Should My Dog Eat per Day?

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published September 5, 2026 · 8 min read

"How many calories should my dog eat per day?" is one of the most searched feeding questions in pet care — and the honest answer is that there is no single number. A growing eight-week-old puppy, a spayed couch-loving Beagle, and a Border Collie running agility three times a week can weigh the same and need wildly different amounts of energy. But the question does have a precise, calculable answer once you supply the right inputs: weight, life stage, reproductive status, and activity. This article gives you the framework veterinarians use, real calorie ranges by weight, and a practical method for telling whether the amount you're feeding is actually right for your dog.

Quick answer: A healthy neutered adult dog needs roughly 1.6 × RER, where RER = 70 × (weight in kg)0.75. In practice that means about 350 kcal/day for a 10 lb dog, 990 kcal for a 40 lb dog, and 1,960 kcal for a 100 lb dog. Get a personalized figure instantly with the Dog Calorie Calculator.

The Formula Behind Every Recommendation

Veterinary nutrition starts from the resting energy requirement (RER) — the energy a dog burns doing nothing at all. The standard formula is RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. That exponent matters: energy needs rise with metabolic body mass, not total mass, which is why a dog twice the size of another needs about 1.7 times the calories, not double.

From RER, a multiplier reflects how the dog actually lives. The commonly used factors are 1.6 for a neutered adult with normal activity, 1.8 for an intact adult, about 1.4 for less active seniors, 2.0–3.0 for growing puppies, and 0.8–1.0 for supervised weight loss. Multiply RER by the right factor and you have a defensible daily target. Here is what that looks like across three common profiles:

Body weight Neutered adult (×1.6) Intact adult (×1.8) Weight loss (×1.0)
10 lb (4.5 kg)~350 kcal~390 kcal~220 kcal
25 lb (11.3 kg)~680 kcal~765 kcal~425 kcal
40 lb (18.2 kg)~990 kcal~1,110 kcal~615 kcal
60 lb (27.2 kg)~1,335 kcal~1,500 kcal~835 kcal
100 lb (45.5 kg)~1,960 kcal~2,205 kcal~1,225 kcal

Look at the spread within a single weight: a 40 lb intact dog needs nearly twice the calories of a 40 lb dog on a weight-loss program. This is exactly why one-size answers — and the generic cup ranges on food bags — fail so many dogs. Life Stage Changes the Math

Puppies are the biggest exception to adult rules. During growth, energy demand per pound of body weight is enormous: puppies under four months may need about three times their RER, and even adolescent pups need roughly twice an adult's relative intake. Growth also requires the correct calcium-to-phosphorus balance, which is why puppies should eat a food formulated for their size class — large-breed puppy formulas in particular control growth rate to protect developing joints.

Seniors usually drift downward, not upward. Muscle mass declines and activity often drops after middle age, so many senior dogs need 10–20% fewer calories than they did at four years old. The trap is habit: owners keep filling the same bowl for a dog whose engine has slowed, and weight creeps up year after year.

Pregnant and lactating females are the other extreme. A nursing mother may need two to four times her normal maintenance energy — and in practice should have food available freely rather than portioned, because the demand fluctuates litter to litter and hour to hour.

Activity: The Variable Owners Underestimate

The ×1.6 baseline assumes a genuinely average dog — a couple of walks a day, normal play, nothing structured. Real life varies more than that. A dog that joins a 5-mile run several times a week, hikes on weekends, or trains in a sport can burn hundreds of extra calories per day and legitimately eat at ×1.8 or beyond. Conversely, a dog that spends the workday on the sofa may need closer to ×1.2–1.4, especially if it is already carrying extra weight.

A useful mental model: think of your dog's week, not its best day. If three days were energetic and four were lazy, feed slightly above baseline and let treats cover the active days. If every day is a trail day, the multiplier earns its raise.

The 10% Treat Rule

Whatever the daily target turns out to be, veterinary nutritionists recommend that no more than 10% of daily calories come from treats, chews, and table scraps. The reason is practical, not just caloric: extras dilute the nutritional completeness of the diet. Kibble is formulated to supply balanced vitamins and minerals at its calorie level; a stream of jerky and cheese is not.

For a 40 lb dog eating ~990 kcal/day, that means the entire treat budget is about 99 kcal — roughly three small biscuits or a single strip of jerky. On heavy-training days, the fix is arithmetic, not guilt: reduce meal portions by the treat calories, or switch to part of the dog's daily kibble ration as training rewards.

Signs the amount is wrong: Ribs you can't feel with light pressure, a waist you can't see from above, and gradual weight gain between vet visits all point to overfeeding. Visible ribs, hip bones, and a sharply tucked abdomen point to underfeeding. Score your dog's body condition at home with the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator.

Feed the Dog in Front of You

The calorie formula produces a starting hypothesis, and your dog's body runs the experiment. Here is the practical loop that works:

  1. Calculate the starting target for weight, life stage, and activity — or use a breed-specific estimate if your dog's breed runs hot or cold metabolically.
  2. Weigh your dog every two weeks under consistent conditions (same scale, same time of day).
  3. Adjust by about 10% if weight is trending the wrong way over a month. Bigger cuts backfire — rapid restriction in dogs triggers the same rebound problems crash diets cause in people.
  4. Recheck after changes in life stage. A dog that was just spayed or neutered needs roughly 10–20% fewer calories within weeks of the procedure. The puppy you calculated for in June is a different animal by December.

And remember that calories are only half of intake — hydration matters daily too. A dog's typical water need is around 1 ounce per pound of body weight, which you can check with the Dog Water Intake Calculator.

The Bottom Line

Most adult dogs land between 25 and 30 kcal per pound of body weight per day — about 350 kcal for a 10 lb dog, 990 kcal for a 40 lb dog, and 1,960 kcal for a 100 lb dog — but the honest answer always depends on life stage, reproductive status, and activity. Compute a starting number, hold treats to 10%, weigh your dog every couple of weeks, and adjust by 10% based on what you feel along the ribs and see at the waistline. The bowl serves the dog, not the other way around — and when in doubt, your veterinarian can help you turn a good estimate into a precise plan.
