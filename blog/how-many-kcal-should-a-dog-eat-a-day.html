How Many kcal Should a Dog Eat a Day?

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 10, 2026 · 8 min read

Ask the internet how many calories a dog needs and you get one answer per weight band, as though body weight were the only variable. Ask a veterinary nutritionist and you get a formula, an activity factor and a caveat about the individual dog. Both describe the same thing, but the second explains why the same 40 lb dog can legitimately need 740 kcal in one household and 1,230 in another.

Quick answer: A typical neutered adult dog needs about 1.6 × its resting energy requirement, where RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. That is roughly 1,500 kcal a day for a 70 lb Labrador and about 350 kcal for a 10 lb dog. Because the exponent is 0.75 rather than 1, needs per kilogram fall as dogs get bigger — about 90 kcal/kg at 5 lb against roughly 43 kcal/kg at 100 lb. kcal, Calorie, cal: Getting the Unit Right First

Before the arithmetic, settle the units. A "calorie" with a lowercase c is the tiny unit of physics: the energy to raise one gram of water by one degree Celsius. The "Calorie" with a capital C used on nutrition labels is a kilocalorie — a thousand of the small ones — abbreviated kcal. When a bag says a food contains 380 Calories per cup, it contains 380 kcal per cup. Every nutrition number in this article is in kcal.

The distinction matters when you compare figures. A label listing "3,650 kcal ME/kg" and one listing "3,650 Calories per kilogram" describe an identical food. But kcal per kilogram for the food and kcal per day for the dog measure different things, and dividing one by the other without checking units is the most common arithmetic error in home feeding plans.

The Two-Step Formula Vets Use

Clinical feeding plans start from resting energy requirement, the energy a dog would burn at complete rest in a thermoneutral environment. It is calculated from body weight alone:

RER (kcal/day) = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75

To convert pounds to kilograms, divide by 2.2. Then multiply RER by an activity factor reflecting the dog's life stage, reproductive status and workload. That product is maintenance energy requirement, or MER, and it is the number you actually feed.

MER (kcal/day) = activity factor × RER

The exponent does important work. Because RER scales with weight to the 0.75 power rather than proportionally, a dog twice as heavy needs only about 1.68 times the energy, not twice. That single fact explains most of the confusion around per-kilogram advice, and why a Great Dane eats less per pound than a Chihuahua. Our guide to the resting energy requirement formula covers the derivation and the three mistakes that skew it.

Two entries carry warnings. A weight-loss plan uses a factor of 1.0, but calculated from the dog's ideal weight rather than its current one — using current weight is the classic mistake that makes a diet look safe while delivering far too much food. And puppy factors apply to expected adult weight, so growing dogs need rechecking rather than a fixed number for months at a time.

Why the Bag's Feeding Guide Usually Overfeeds

Labels are legally required to carry feeding directions, and those directions are written to cover a broad range of dogs safely rather than to match yours. Two biases push them high: the guides tend to assume a more active dog than the average pet, often an intact or lightly working animal, and they are designed so that no dog is underfed on the manufacturer's food.

The result is that plenty of dogs fed strictly to the bag gain weight. Spaying or neutering alone commonly lowers energy needs by 20-30%, and the change often arrives with no adjustment to the bowl. If your dog sits at the top of the bag's weight band, start at the lower end and let body condition decide whether to move up.

The Fastest Sanity Check: kcal per Kilogram

If you want one number to carry in your head, use kcal per kilogram of body weight. For a typical neutered adult at normal activity, the practical band runs from about 90 kcal/kg in toy breeds down to roughly 43 kcal/kg in the largest dogs — the right-hand column above. A 40 lb dog sits near 54.

That pattern tells you which way to correct an implausible number. A claim that a 5 lb Chihuahua needs 45 kcal/kg was almost certainly lifted from large-breed advice and is roughly half what the dog needs. A 100 lb dog fed at 90 kcal/kg is being given double its requirement. Per-kilogram needs are not constant, so a per-kilogram rule is only safe when you also know the size class.

Checking the Number Against Your Dog

Formulas describe populations; the dog in front of you is the measurement. Body condition scoring takes about fifteen seconds by hand. Run your fingers along the ribcage: at an ideal score the ribs are easy to feel under a thin fat layer but not visible. Look down from above for a waist behind the ribs, and from the side for a tuck where the abdomen rises. The Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator turns that into a score.

Then close the loop. Feed your calculated number consistently for three weeks — same measuring method every meal, which is where the cups-per-day conversion earns its keep — and re-weigh or re-score. If weight is drifting the wrong way, change the ration by about 10% and run another three weeks. Weight change on a truly unchanged ration, or a sudden shift in appetite, is veterinary rather than dietary.

When the Number Changes

The most common reason a formerly correct number stops working is neutering, which typically drops requirements by a fifth to a third and explains why so many dogs gain weight in the year after surgery. Age lowers needs toward 1.4 × RER for a senior, cold weather raises them for thin-coated dogs kept outdoors, and activity is the most volatile of all — a hunting dog can run from 4 × RER in season to little more than 1.6 off-season.

Disease moves the number too, which is why unexplained weight change on an unchanged diet warrants a veterinary visit rather than a manual adjustment. Hypothyroidism, more common in some breeds, typically presents as weight gain, lethargy and a poor coat on stable intake. Plans for dogs with kidney, heart or endocrine disease, and for pregnant or lactating bitches, should be set with your veterinarian. The Bottom Line

There is no single kcal figure per body weight, because the answer depends on the activity factor as much as on mass. Start from RER = 70 × (kg)0.75, multiply by the factor matching your dog's life stage and workload, and expect roughly 90 kcal/kg for a toy breed down to about 43 kcal/kg for a giant. Feed that consistently for three weeks, then let body condition decide the next 10%.
