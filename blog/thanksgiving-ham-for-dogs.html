Can Dogs Eat Thanksgiving Ham? The 1,010-Milligram Answer

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published November 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Three ounces of cured ham, one thick slice, carries roughly 1,010 milligrams of sodium. The same three ounces of plain roast turkey breast carries about 65.

Both will be on the table in thirteen days. The dog underneath it does not distinguish between them, and the arithmetic that decides whether either one matters runs on body weight rather than on which meat your family calls the healthy one.

Quick answer: Ham is not poisonous to dogs the way chocolate or raisins are. It is a salt dose, and a 20-pound dog needs about 21 ounces of cured ham, more than a pound and a quarter, before sodium toxicosis becomes realistic. A few bites under the table do not come close. The dogs that get hurt are small, have finished off a leftover half ham, and do not have water in front of them.

One Ounce of Ham, One Pound of Turkey

Ham is pork cured in salt, and the salt stays in the meat. USDA nutrient records put roasted cured ham near 1,193 milligrams of sodium per 100 grams. Unsalted roast turkey breast runs 50 to 100.

The ratio is easier to hold onto in ounces: one ounce of cured ham carries about the sodium of one pound of plain turkey. That is 337 milligrams against 345.

Sliced deli turkey is not the safe column. Injection and curing push it to 600 or 900 milligrams per 100 grams, close enough to ham that the swap buys almost nothing, and a basted bird has been injected with salt before it ever reaches the oven. The low-sodium version is the one you roasted yourself, carved off the bone.

Two Grams Per Kilogram Is Where Signs Start

Merck gives the first clinical signs at 2 to 3 grams of sodium chloride per kilogram of body weight, with an acute lethal dose near 4 grams per kilogram. Salt is 39 percent sodium, so 2 grams of salt per kilogram is 787 milligrams of sodium per kilogram of dog.

For a 20-pound dog that is 7,140 milligrams of sodium, or 21 ounces of cured ham. The lethal line sits at twice the dose, about two and a half pounds. A 10-pound dog gets there on 10 ounces, and a Chihuahua that inherits a ham on Friday morning has a real problem.

Country ham and dry-cured ham carry about double the sodium per ounce, which halves every figure in the ham columns. The same 20-pound dog is in trouble at 10 ounces rather than 21.

Two conditions push those numbers the wrong way: a dog that has been short on water, and a dog whose kidneys are already failing. Merck is direct about the first. Salt toxicosis is unlikely while sodium regulation is intact and fresh water is available, which is a fair assumption on a Thursday and a poor one on a road trip.

Water Is the Second Half of the Dose

Take the water away and a survivable dose becomes an unlivable one. The water disappears for ordinary reasons. The bowl gets moved so the folding table fits and nobody puts it back. The dog rides six hours in a car and drinks twice. A crowded kitchen leaves the bowl behind a chair nobody shifts.

A 40-pound dog normally takes in 31 to 37 ounces a day at 50 to 60 milliliters per kilogram. A salty meal raises that requirement, and urine output with it. Three ounces of ham is more than half the 1,920-milligram upper limit for a 20-pound dog eating 510 calories, and that dog needs water it cannot ask for.

Never withhold water from a dog that has eaten salt, not even to protect the rug. Restriction is what turns a high-sodium meal into hypernatremia. A dog already showing neurological signs is a hospital case; the correction has to be slow.

Three Dogs That Should Not Get Any

A dog with congestive heart failure is usually on furosemide, often on benazepril, and eating a prescription diet held under 0.2 percent sodium on a dry matter basis. The limit exists because sodium pulls fluid back into lungs the drug just cleared. Three ounces of ham spends a week of that budget.

A dog with chronic kidney disease past IRIS stage 2 cannot shed a sodium load efficiently, and the blood pressure that follows damages the same kidneys. A dog with a pancreatitis history has two reasons to skip cured meat, since fat and salt arrive together. Add dogs on long-term steroids, and dogs with liver disease and ascites.

Pick by situation:
  • Healthy dog, no diagnosis: 1 ounce of plain roast turkey breast off the bone, about 40 calories and 22 milligrams of sodium.
  • Dog under 15 pounds: half an ounce, about 21 calories, put in the bowl rather than handed down.
  • Heart, kidney or pancreatitis diagnosis: the prescription food, weighed, and nothing from the table.
  • The drippings, the glaze and the pan: the sink, before the plates come off.

Thanksgiving Ham Questions

Is one small piece of ham safe for a dog?

For a healthy 20-pound dog, a one-inch cube weighs about 10 grams and carries roughly 120 milligrams, 6 percent of the day's ceiling. That is not a poisoning. It is also not free: four relatives handing over a cube each puts the dog at a quarter of the ceiling before the bird is carved.

My dog ate the ham bone. Is the salt the problem?

Not first. A cured ham bone is salty, but it is also cooked, which makes it brittle, and coated in fat. Splinters and pancreatitis arrive before any sodium effect does. Do not induce vomiting over a bone; the return trip is where the damage happens.

How fast do salt poisoning signs appear?

Vomiting usually comes first, within a few hours, then diarrhea, heavy thirst, weakness, a wobbly gait, tremors and in severe cases seizures. A dog that looks normal at eight in the evening can be unsteady by eleven. Decontamination works best inside two hours, which is why you call early instead of watching.

The ham is not the villain. Two things are: a dog that finishes a pound of it, and a dog with nothing to drink afterward. Thirteen days is enough time to move a bowl, buy a second for the car, and decide which relatives may feed the dog.

Drive to the emergency hospital tonight if a dog that ate a large amount of cured meat vomits and then wobbles, presses its head into a wall, tremors or seizes, or cannot keep water down. Bring the ham's packaging: the panel gives sodium per serving, and both poison lines will want that number against the dog's weight. ASPCA Animal Poison Control is at (888) 426-4435 and Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661, both around the clock for a per-case fee. A dog that ate a few bites and is drinking normally can be watched at home with the bowl refilled.
