How Much to Feed My Dog to Lose Weight

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published September 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Your veterinarian says the dog needs to drop four pounds. A calculator says the daily target is 530 kcal. Neither number tells you what goes in the bowl tomorrow morning — and that gap between a calorie target and an actual measured portion is where most home weight-loss plans quietly fall apart. Owners rarely fail for lack of discipline. They fail because a "cup" of kibble is not a unit of measurement, because treats never get counted, and because nobody recalculates as the dog shrinks.

Quick answer: Find out what your dog actually eats in calories today, treats included. Then feed roughly 20–30% less — equivalent to RER × 1.0 at your dog's ideal weight, and never below RER itself. Weigh the food in grams rather than scooping cups, because a loosely filled cup can carry 25% more kibble than a level one. Get your number from the Dog Calorie Calculator using the "Weight Loss Program" life stage.

Why "How Much" Is Harder Than "How Many Calories"

The arithmetic of weight loss is simple: feed fewer calories than the dog burns. The hard part is the conversion, because calories describe the food while portions describe what you control. The same 600 kcal target is 188 grams of a 3,200 kcal/kg light formula and 136 grams of a 4,400 kcal/kg performance diet — a quarter of the bowl. Switch foods mid-diet, keep scooping the same volume, and the plan silently breaks.

Step 1: Find Out What Your Dog Eats Today

You cannot reduce something you have never measured. Count every calorie that crosses your dog's lips for three days — meals, biscuits, dental chews, table scraps, the peanut butter wrapped around a pill, and the treats handed out on a walk. The label supplies the missing piece: metabolizable energy appears as kcal/kg, kcal/cup, or both. On some imported foods it is listed in kilojoules — divide by 4.184.

Two peanut-butter spoons and a dental chew is 270 kcal — nearly half the daily allowance, delivered as "just a little something." The standard ceiling is 10% of daily calories from treats, which on a 600 kcal target is 60 kcal. If your household is far above that line, fixing the treats is the easiest available deficit, because removing them costs no nutrition.

Step 2: Decide How Big the Reduction Should Be

Two rules of thumb converge here, and the safer one should win when they disagree. The first is a flat percentage: feed 20–30% less than the dog eats today. The second is the veterinary-standard energy calculation — RER × 1.0 at ideal weight, where RER = 70 × (body weight in kilograms)0.75. That is what the Dog Calorie Calculator returns when you select the Weight Loss Program life stage, and it is better because it scales with the dog rather than with whatever was in the bowl last month. A dog whose ideal weight is 40 lb (18.1 kg) has an RER of about 615 kcal per day; at an ideal weight of 20 lb (9.1 kg), it is about 366 kcal. If you are unsure of the ideal weight, the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator will give you a defensible target using body condition scoring.

The one line you must not cross is RER itself. Feeding below resting energy requirement means supplying less food than the dog needs to run its own organs, and a maintenance diet cannot be stretched that far without shortchanging protein, vitamins and minerals. Diets that go below RER exist, but they are therapeutic formulas with concentrated nutrition, and they belong under veterinary supervision.

Respect the size of the gap, too. When the target is more than about 30% below current intake, step down over two to four weeks instead of dropping straight to it. The average rate of loss is the same, with less hunger and less muscle lost.

Step 3: Convert the Target Into Grams

Formula: grams per day = daily kcal ÷ (kcal per kg ÷ 1,000)
Example — 600 kcal target on a 3,600 kcal/kg food: 600 ÷ 3.6 = 167 g per day, split into two meals of about 83 g.

What makes this worth doing rather than eyeballing is how far the answer moves between foods. The table shows the same 600 kcal target across the energy densities you are most likely to meet in a pet store.

A digital kitchen scale costs less than a bag of food and is the most effective piece of equipment in this process. Weigh in grams, not ounces, not cups. Measurements of how owners portion kibble consistently find overfeeding with a cup, with errors in the 20–48% range depending on scoop size and filling — always in the direction that stalls a diet. If you prefer cups, calibrate once: weigh a level scoop three times, average it, and divide your daily grams by that figure.

Two meals is the default for adult dogs, because it keeps the stomach from staying empty long enough to trigger scavenging or bile vomiting; three smaller meals work if one leaves the dog frantic. Hold back roughly 10% of the allowance for training, and subtract those calories from the meals rather than adding them on top. Hunger is the main reason owners abandon a plan that is working, so protect volume while cutting energy: green beans, cucumber, courgette or carrot add bulk for very few calories, a spoonful of warm water slows eating, and a slow-feeder bowl or snuffle mat stretches the same calories across more minutes.

Step 4: Weigh Every Two Weeks and Recalculate

The target is a moving number, not a setting. As a dog loses weight, its RER falls, so the calories that produced loss at 48 lb no longer produce loss at 40 lb. A dog moving from 48 lb to its 40 lb ideal falls from roughly 705 to about 615 kcal per day — feed the original target all the way down and the deficit shrinks toward zero, which is the plateau owners usually blame on a "slow metabolism." Weigh on the same scale every two weeks and recalculate after every 5% of body weight lost. The safe rate is 1–2% of body weight per week, which for a 50 lb dog is half a pound to a pound.

If a carefully measured, recently recalculated deficit produces no change over four to six weeks, stop adjusting the food and see a veterinarian. Hypothyroidism and Cushing's disease are real, diagnosable causes of refractory weight gain, and no portion size will fix either. Once the dog reaches a body condition score of 4–5 out of 9, switch off the deficit and move to maintenance — for a neutered adult, roughly RER × 1.6 at the new weight — then keep weighing monthly for six months.

When Not to Do This Alone

Home portion control suits a healthy adult dog that needs to lose less than about 20% of its body weight. It is not appropriate for puppies, pregnant or nursing dogs, dogs with diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease or pancreatitis, or any dog needing to lose a large amount of weight. Whenever the required intake drops below RER, the plan needs a purpose-formulated therapeutic diet and a veterinarian.

The Bottom Line

How much to feed a dog to lose weight is a four-number answer: what the dog eats today, the lower target from RER × 1.0 at ideal weight, the grams that equals on your bag of food, and the weight on the scale two weeks from now. Measure in grams, keep treats inside 10%, stay at or above RER, and recalculate after every 5% lost.
