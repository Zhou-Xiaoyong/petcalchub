Signs of Chocolate Poisoning in Dogs: What to Watch For

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published August 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Sometimes you know exactly what happened — the wrapper is on the floor and the dog looks guilty. But often you don't. You come home, the dog seems "off," and hours later you find a shredded gift bag under the sofa. In those cases, recognising the signs of chocolate poisoning is what triggers the phone call that saves the day. Chocolate toxicosis has an unusually predictable symptom progression, and knowing which sign belongs to which stage tells you how far along the process is and how urgently you need help.

Quick answer: Early signs (2-6 hours) are gastrointestinal — vomiting, diarrhoea, drooling, and a sudden thirst. Mid-stage signs (6-12 hours) are stimulant effects — restlessness, panting, and a racing heart. Late signs (12 hours and beyond) are neurological and cardiac — tremors, stiffness, collapse, and seizures. If you know how much was eaten, run the numbers with the Chocolate Toxicity Calculator rather than waiting for symptoms to appear.

Why the Signs Arrive on a Schedule

Chocolate poisoning is caused by theobromine, a methylxanthine that dogs metabolise far more slowly than people do. In dogs the elimination half-life is roughly 17.5 hours, compared with about 7 hours in humans. Because clearance is so slow, blood levels keep climbing for hours after the last bite and then decline gradually over a day or more.

That pharmacology is why the symptom picture unfolds in stages rather than all at once. The gut reacts first, because that is where the chocolate physically is. Then, as theobromine reaches the bloodstream and crosses into the central nervous system and heart muscle, the stimulant effects take over. The most dangerous signs are usually the last to appear — which is exactly why a dog that "seems fine" two hours in is not necessarily safe.

Stage 1: Early Signs (2-6 Hours)

The first signs are almost always digestive, and they are easy to dismiss as ordinary dietary indiscretion:

  • Vomiting, sometimes with visible chocolate or wrapper fragments. This is the single most common first sign.
  • Diarrhoea, often loose and dark, occasionally with a chocolate smell.
  • Excessive drooling or repeated lip-licking, reflecting nausea.
  • Increased thirst and urination. Theobromine is a mild diuretic, so a dog that suddenly empties the water bowl and asks to go out repeatedly is showing a real pharmacological effect, not just an upset stomach.
  • Abdominal discomfort — a hunched posture, reluctance to be picked up, or a tense belly.

A useful clue: plain dietary upset rarely comes with a dramatic thirst spike. Vomiting plus obvious polydipsia in a dog with access to chocolate is a meaningful combination.

Stage 2: Stimulant Signs (6-12 Hours)

As theobromine concentrations peak, the picture changes from "sick stomach" to "wired." Owners often describe a dog that cannot settle:

  • Hyperactivity and restlessness — pacing, circling, an inability to lie down, whining.
  • Panting that is out of proportion to the temperature or activity level.
  • Elevated heart rate (tachycardia). A resting heart rate well above the normal 60-140 beats per minute range for dogs is a warning sign; you can count it by feeling the femoral pulse on the inner thigh.
  • Muscle twitching — fine tremors, most visible in the head, shoulders, and flanks.
  • Dilated pupils and heightened sensitivity to sound and touch.

This stage is where many owners first realise something is genuinely wrong. It is also the point at which veterinary monitoring becomes appropriate for almost any dog, regardless of how the dose calculation turned out.

Stage 3: Severe Signs (12-24+ Hours)

At high doses, cardiac and neurological effects dominate and constitute a medical emergency:

  • Pronounced muscle tremors and rigidity, sometimes progressing to a stiff, unsteady gait (ataxia).
  • Cardiac arrhythmias — an irregular, skipping, or very fast pulse.
  • Hyperthermia. Sustained tremors generate heat; a body temperature above roughly 103°F (39.4°C) adds a second layer of danger.
  • Seizures — from brief focal twitching to full generalised convulsions.
  • Weakness or collapse, and in the worst cases cardiovascular failure.

Because theobromine clears so slowly, this window can extend well past 24 hours in a large ingestion — clinical signs lasting 24 to 72 hours are documented.

Signs Mapped to Dose

Veterinarians grade chocolate toxicosis by milligrams of theobromine per kilogram of body weight. The bands below line up closely with the stages above, which is why calculating the dose lets you anticipate symptoms instead of reacting to them.

Approx. dose Expected signs Typical action
Under 20 mg/kgOften none; possible mild stomach upset from fat and sugarMonitor at home
20-40 mg/kgVomiting, diarrhoea, thirst, urination, restlessnessCall your vet or a poison hotline
40-60 mg/kgTachycardia, arrhythmias, tremors, marked agitationVeterinary exam and monitoring
Above 60 mg/kgSeizures, severe arrhythmias, hyperthermia, collapseEmergency care immediately

These bands are guidance, not guarantees. Puppies, seniors, small breeds, and dogs with existing heart disease, epilepsy, or pancreatitis can show serious signs at lower doses. Signs That Are Easy to Misread

Several presentations regularly cause confusion. Rich chocolate is very high in fat, so a dog can develop vomiting and abdominal pain from fat-induced gastroenteritis or pancreatitis even when the theobromine dose is trivially low — the symptoms look alarming but the mechanism is different. Conversely, an anxious dog that paces at night may simply be anxious, but pacing that begins within hours of an unsupervised period near a chocolate stash deserves a closer look.

Wrappers, foil, and plastic add a mechanical hazard. A dog that swallowed foil along with the chocolate can develop obstruction signs — repeated retching without producing anything, a painful abdomen, and no stool — that need imaging regardless of the toxin calculation.

It is also worth remembering that other kitchen items produce overlapping symptoms. Sugar-free products containing xylitol cause weakness and collapse from hypoglycaemia within 30 minutes, a much faster onset than chocolate; our Xylitol Toxicity Calculator covers that scenario. Grapes and raisins cause vomiting followed by lethargy and reduced urination as kidney injury develops, which the Grape & Raisin Toxicity Calculator addresses. If your dog raided a mixed snack platter, more than one toxin may be in play.

Red Flags: Go to the Emergency Vet Now

Skip the wait-and-see approach entirely if you observe any of the following: a seizure of any length, collapse or inability to stand, a heart rate that is obviously racing or irregular, continuous whole-body tremors, a temperature above 104°F (40°C), or repeated vomiting that prevents the dog from keeping water down. Puppies under six months, toy breeds, pregnant dogs, and dogs with known cardiac or seizure disorders should be assessed sooner rather than later even with mild signs.

Two hotlines are staffed by veterinary toxicologists around the clock: the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 and the Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661. Both charge a consultation fee and both can advise your vet directly. Bring the packaging, note the time of ingestion, and do not induce vomiting on your own — it is unsafe in a dog that is already tremoring, seizing, or flat-faced.

The Bottom Line

Chocolate poisoning announces itself in a recognisable order: stomach signs first, stimulant signs second, cardiac and neurological signs last. Because theobromine takes so long to clear, the absence of symptoms in the first few hours is reassuring but not conclusive. The most reliable strategy is to treat known ingestion as the trigger for action, calculate the dose immediately, and use the symptom stages as a monitoring checklist rather than a starting gun.
