Dog Drank the Turkey Brine? The 2 g/kg Salt Line

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Wednesday night, the turkey goes into the brine bag on the bottom shelf, the bag has a pinhole near the seam, and by 6 a.m. there is a puddle of salty water on the glass and a dog with a wet muzzle. Nobody thinks of that puddle as poison. It is salt water at roughly twice the concentration of the ocean, and a 25 lb dog crosses the veterinary threshold for salt toxicosis at about 10 fluid ounces of it.

Quick answer: clinical signs of salt toxicosis in dogs start around 2 to 3 g of salt per kilogram of body weight, and the acute lethal dose is near 4 g/kg (Merck Veterinary Manual). A wet brine mixed at one cup of table salt per gallon runs about 7.7% salt, so 10 fl oz puts a 25 lb dog at the low end and 20 fl oz puts a 50 lb dog there. Call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 or your vet before you do anything else, and do not let him drink a full bowl of water to rinse it out.

Which Salt Went Into the Pot

Brine recipes are written in cups, and a cup is a terrible unit for salt. One cup of table salt weighs about 290 grams. One cup of Diamond Crystal kosher salt weighs about 134 grams, because the flakes are hollow and the crystals pack loosely. The same recipe, followed exactly, produces liquids that differ by more than a factor of two.

That difference decides your dog's dose. At one cup per gallon, table salt gives you a 7.7% solution. Diamond Crystal gives you 3.5%, which is seawater. Poison control will ask which salt you used, so have the box in hand before you dial.

A 12 lb bird wet-brined in a gallon and a half leaves quarts of liquid behind, and a Labrador can empty two cups of it in under a minute.

The table converts Merck's per-kilogram thresholds into brine volume at 7.7%. Volumes are approximate because recipes vary, but the shape of the problem holds: small dogs need very little.

A 25 lb dog needs around 20 fl oz of water in 24 hours, which the Dog Water Intake Calculator will confirm for his exact weight. Ten ounces of brine is half that day, delivered as salt instead of water.

Why the Water Bowl Comes Second

Sodium pulls water toward itself. When serum sodium climbs fast, water leaves the cells to dilute it, and the cells that suffer first are in the brain. Merck describes the sequence plainly: cerebral dehydration, neurons shrinking, the brain pulling away from the skull, small vessels tearing. That is what drives the wobbling and the seizures.

The body adapts by generating its own osmolytes so brain cells can hold water. That adaptation is why the correction has to be slow. Pour a bowl of fresh water into a dog whose brain has spent hours adjusting to a high sodium load and water rushes back into those cells, and the brain swells. Vets avoid this by lowering serum sodium 0.5 to 1.0 mEq/L per hour and replacing no more than half the free water deficit in 24 hours.

A normal dog runs 140 to 150 mEq/L. Above 160 mEq/L, Merck treats it as salt toxicosis. Small sips are fine. A gallon bowl on the floor is not the favor it looks like, and withholding water entirely is not either.

The First Hour, in Order

Move the bag out of reach and photograph the salt box you used. Note the time you found him and the rough volume missing from the bag. Weigh him if you can, since every threshold here is per kilogram.

Then call. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center runs (888) 426-4435 around the clock and Pet Poison Helpline is (855) 764-7661. Both charge a consultation fee and both are staffed on Thanksgiving Day. Have the salt type, volume, weight, and clock time ready before you dial, because those four facts are the whole case.

Skip the home remedies. Salt water as an emetic is the wrong tool here, and Merck notes it is no longer recommended as one. Hydrogen peroxide has its own problems and does nothing about a toxin already absorbed.

What the Hospital Does

If the exposure was recent and the dog is not yet showing signs, a vet may induce vomiting and give activated charcoal. Once signs have started, the work is supportive: IV fluids calculated to bring sodium down gradually, diazepam or another anti-seizure drug if he is seizing, and mannitol if brain swelling is suspected.

Most dogs treated early do well. The ones who fare badly usually arrived after a seizure, or had sodium dropped too fast by well-meaning home care. Expect a stay measured in a day or two rather than an afternoon, and a bill to match. Our Pet Insurance Cost Calculator shows what a two-night hospitalization does to a premium.

Where the Salt Hides on Thanksgiving Morning

The brine bag is the obvious one. Three others are easier to miss.

The dry-brine drip. A dry brine at one tablespoon of salt per five pounds puts four tablespoons, about 72 grams, on a 20 lb bird. It draws moisture out, and that moisture collects in the roasting pan as a concentrated puddle. For a 10 lb dog, 9 grams is the 2 g/kg line, so a few licks of that pan can carry him past it before the turkey reaches the oven.

A self-basting bird. Turkeys labeled "basted" or "self-basting" are injected with a salt solution that USDA rules allow to make up as much as 3% of the bird's weight. Drippings from one of those are saltier than drippings from a natural turkey, which matters if you were planning to spoon them over kibble as a treat. Don't.

The bucket on the porch. Cold-weather brining often happens in a cooler or a five-gallon bucket, exactly the height a dog's head reaches. A lid that snaps beats a lid that sits. If the bucket has been out there since Tuesday, so has the spill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much turkey brine is dangerous for a dog?

At 7.7% salt, about 4 fl oz for a 10 lb dog, 10 fl oz for a 25 lb dog, and 20 fl oz for a 50 lb dog to reach the 2 g/kg mark where signs begin. Double those and he is near the 4 g/kg lethal dose.

He drank brine and seems fine. Can I just watch him?

Watch him, but call first. Signs usually appear within one to four hours and the early ones look like a stomach ache: thirst, drooling, vomiting, loose stool. The dog who looks normal at hour two can be the one who seizes at hour six.

Should I make him throw up?

Not on your own, and never with salt water. Salt as an emetic adds to the sodium load you are already managing, and Pet Poison Helpline advises against it outright. Induction only makes sense in a narrow window, with a vet's guidance, and in a dog not already showing neurological signs.

Red Lines

Go to an emergency hospital now if he is wobbly, trembling, confused, vomiting repeatedly, or has had a seizure, and go now if the volume he took sits at or above the 2 g/kg row for his weight. Do not wait for your regular clinic, since the holiday hours gap runs longer than most people expect. Take the salt box with you, because the concentration is half the diagnosis.

If he is bright, drinking normally, and he only licked a weak brine, you still have a phone call to make, and you have a bag to move to a shelf he cannot reach. Tie it inside a second bag or put it in a lidded container. The rest of this page exists for the version of Thursday where that did not happen.
