Dog Ate the Raisins Out of the Stuffing? The 2.8 g/kg Line

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

At 4:15 on Thanksgiving afternoon the stuffing bowl is on the counter with the raisins picked out of it, and the dog is licking his chops by the back door. A cup of raisins weighs about 145 grams. For a 10 lb dog that is 32 grams per kilogram, more than eleven times the lowest amount reported to cause kidney failure.

Here is the part that catches people. He will look completely normal at midnight. The kidney injury shows up in the bloodwork a day or two later, which is exactly when the family has stopped watching him.

Quick answer: raisins have no safe dose, so there is no number that means fine. The lowest figure reported in the veterinary literature is 2.8 g/kg of raisins, which is about 25 raisins for a 10 lb dog. If it has been under two hours, call your vet or poison control now and let them make the call on inducing vomiting. If it has been longer, the job is 72 hours of watching urine output plus a creatinine recheck. The Grape and Raisin Toxicity Calculator turns what he ate into grams per kilogram.

What Was Actually in the Stuffing

Most stuffing recipes call for half a cup of raisins across eight servings, which is 72 grams of raisins in the whole dish. A dog who cleans out the bowl has eaten all of it.

The other path is quieter. Guests bring a trail mix bowl, a loaf of raisin bread, a bread pudding. Nobody counts what disappears from a bowl on a coffee table.

Percentages are the share of the 2.8 g/kg line at that weight. A 50 lb dog has to work for it. A 10 lb dog does not, and one slice of bread pudding already passes the line.

The 2.8 g/kg Figure and Who Published It

The number comes from a 2005 review in the Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine: 43 dogs treated for grape and raisin ingestion between 1999 and 2002. The lowest dose that produced acute kidney injury was 19.6 g/kg for grapes and 2.8 g/kg for raisins. Some of those dogs died. Others ate comparable amounts and were fine.

Raisins sit lower because drying removes water and concentrates what the grape carried. Dehydration accounts for roughly fourfold; the published ratio is sevenfold. That gap tells you these are floor values from a small case series, not a conversion table. Treat 2.8 g/kg as where cases start, not where harm starts.

Counts assume 0.5 g per raisin and 5 g per grape. They orient you. They do not grant permission. A dog under the line still belongs on a 72-hour watch, because the dose that matters is what his kidneys absorbed.

Why There Is No Safe Number

The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center treats grapes and raisins as toxic at any amount. In the 43-dog series, dogs that ate similar doses ended up in different places, and no study has found a threshold below which nothing happens.

The leading explanation involves tartaric acid, which dogs metabolize poorly and which is present in grapes. It is still a hypothesis. Tamarind contains tartaric acid at higher levels and is not tied to the same kidney pattern, so the mechanism is not settled.

Same weight, same bowl, different outcomes. That gap is why the calculator describes a dose rather than clearing it. Run the number and hand it to whoever answers the phone.

The First Two Hours

  • Weigh what is left, not what you think he ate. If the bag is open, put it on a kitchen scale. A difference of 40 grams is 80 raisins, and that is a number worth reporting.
  • Call before you clean up. ASPCA Animal Poison Control is (888) 426-4435 and Pet Poison Helpline is (855) 764-7661. Both charge a consultation fee and both staff the holiday.
  • Do not reach for hydrogen peroxide. It causes stomach ulceration, the dose is easy to get wrong in a small dog, and it is the wrong move if he is already vomiting or dull.
  • Induction is a clinic decision. It makes sense inside roughly two hours, in a dog who is alert and not a flat-faced breed. After that the raisins are past the stomach and vomiting gains little.
  • Skip the bread, milk, and oil. None of them bind anything here, and a full stomach makes vomiting harder if the vet decides to do it.

The Kidney Clock: 24 to 72 Hours

Vomiting shows up first, usually 6 to 12 hours in. Some dogs vomit once and stop. Others never vomit and just go quiet, which is the more worrying version.

Creatinine lags the injury. A panel drawn at hour 12 can look acceptable in a dog whose kidneys are already shutting down, so one normal result does not end this. The recheck at 48 to 72 hours settles it.

Urine output is the home number that means something: 1 to 2 mL per kilogram per hour, or half a cup to just under a cup a day for a 10 lb dog. Soaking a pee pad and producing nothing are both information. No urine at all, or a flood of pale urine, is the clinic tonight.

What the Clinic Does, and What It Costs

Decontamination if you are early, then fluids at two to three times maintenance for 48 to 72 hours, anti-nausea medication, and a urine output record. Dialysis exists for dogs who go anuric, in a handful of cities.

An emergency exam runs $200 to $400 on a holiday. Bloodwork with a urinalysis is $200 to $350, and most cases repeat it two or three times. Two to three days of IV fluids and monitoring lands between $800 and $2,500. Dialysis starts near $3,000 and can pass $8,000.

Insurance will not help with a policy bought this week. Most plans hold a 14-day waiting period for new conditions, so coverage bought on Wednesday does nothing on Thursday.

What to Do Tonight, in Order

  1. Weigh the remaining raisins and write down the time you found the bowl empty.
  2. Run the number, then call your vet or poison control with the dose and the clock in hand.
  3. Measure water in the bowl and note how much he drinks overnight, since thirst changes early.
  4. Book the 48-hour creatinine recheck before you go to bed. Holiday slots fill first.
  5. Move every raisin, trail mix, and fruitcake to a closed cabinet. The second raid is the one that happens while you are at the clinic.

Thanksgiving Stuffing Questions

How many raisins are dangerous for a dog?

The lowest published figure is 2.8 g/kg: 25 raisins for a 10 lb dog, 127 for a 50 lb dog. That is a reporting floor from 43 cases, not a safe limit. Smaller doses have been followed by kidney injury, so the count sets urgency, not permission.

My dog ate one raisin. Is he going to be okay?

Probably. One raisin is 0.5 g, or 0.11 g/kg for a 10 lb dog, about 4% of the lowest reported dose. Probably is not never. Watch water and urine for 72 hours, and ask your vet whether a creatinine check at 48 hours is worth it.

Should I make him vomit if I caught him right away?

Ask first, and only inside two hours. A vet or poison control can tell you whether induction fits this dog, this amount, and this breed. Hydrogen peroxide at home is the wrong tool: the margin between effective and damaging is narrow, and it burns the stomach lining on the way through.

Put the Raisins Out of Reach

The fix costs nothing and happens before the guests arrive. Move the raisins to a closed upper cabinet, keep the trail mix off the coffee table, and tell the person doing the baking that the dog can reach the counter.

The red lines: no urine for 12 hours, vomiting that will not stop, gums that look pale or white, a dog who will not stand, or breath that smells like ammonia. Any of those means you are driving to an emergency hospital, not waiting for Monday. Past 72 hours with normal drinking, normal urination, and a clean creatinine, the window has closed.
