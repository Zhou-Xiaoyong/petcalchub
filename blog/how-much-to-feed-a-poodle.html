How Much to Feed a Poodle: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 28, 2026 · 8 min read

Thirteen grams. That is a level tablespoon of kibble, and it is an entire meal for a 2 lb Toy Poodle puppy at eight weeks old. Four of those a day and the puppy is fed. A six-month Standard Poodle takes 118 grams at one sitting, roughly nine times the same spoon, and both dogs are Poodles.

The breed name tells you almost nothing about the bowl. Size tells you most of it. Age tells you the rest, and the food's own calorie density decides what that final number looks like when it lands in a scoop.

Every number below comes from the same formula that runs behind our Dog Calorie Calculator, applied at three sizes and four life stages.

Quick answer: Work out RER = 70 × (kg)0.75, then apply the right factor for age and size: 3.0 for a puppy under four months, 2.5 from four to six months, 2.0 while it is still growing, 1.6 in the final stretch, and 1.3 to 1.6 once mature depending on the size. A 2 lb Toy puppy at 3.0 lands at 196 kcal and 52 grams a day, split four ways. A 60 lb Standard adult at 1.6 lands at 1,334 kcal and 351 grams, split two ways. Put your own dog's weight into the Dog Calorie Calculator to skip the arithmetic.

The Puppy Ladder Has Four Rungs

A puppy is not a small adult with a bigger appetite. It is building bone and muscle, and that construction work sits on top of ordinary maintenance. Veterinary feeding guides handle this with a staged multiplier rather than one number for the whole first year:

  • Under four months: 3.0 × RER. Growth is fastest here and the ration looks enormous next to the dog.
  • Four to six months: 2.5 × RER. The steepest part of the curve is past.
  • Six months to about 80% of adult weight: 2.0 × RER.
  • Final stretch to maturity: 1.6 × RER, which is close enough to adult that the change is small.

The ladder is where the three sizes split apart. A Toy Poodle hits 80 percent of adult weight around five months, so it clatters down the rungs in a hurry. A Standard sits on the 2.0 rung for most of a year, and an owner who moves that dog onto adult portions at eight months because it looks grown is cutting the ration months too early.

Read the last column, not the daily total. Thirteen grams is one level measuring tablespoon. Nobody scoops thirteen grams twice in a row by eye, and the error on a Toy is the difference between a lean dog and a body condition score of 7. A kitchen scale that reads to one gram costs less than a bag of food and ends the argument.

At the other end, 354 grams at six months is three-quarters of a pound of kibble a day. Owners routinely pour half that because a Standard puppy does not look like it needs that much, then wonder why a growing dog is ribby at eight months. Puppies at peak growth eat more than the adults they become.

When to Drop a Meal

Meal count is a stomach-capacity question, not a calorie question. A young puppy physically cannot hold a day's ration at once, so the same grams get divided more ways. For Poodles the usual schedule looks like this:

  • 8 to 12 weeks: four meals. A 13 g meal is about a tablespoon, which sounds ridiculous and is correct.
  • 12 weeks to 6 months: three meals for Toys and Miniatures, three or four for a Standard still under 20 lb.
  • 6 months on: three meals for Toys and Miniatures, two or three for a Standard. Two is fine once the dog clears 40 lb.
  • Adult: two meals. One large meal a day raises the risk of bloat in a deep-chested Standard and does nothing useful for anyone else.

There is a hard constraint on the Toy end that does not exist for the other two sizes. A puppy under three pounds carries almost no liver glycogen to draw on between meals, so an overnight gap longer than about six hours can drop its blood sugar. That means a late feeding around 11 p.m. and breakfast by 6 a.m. for the first few months, whether or not it is convenient.

A Toy puppy that goes wobbly, drools heavily, or cannot stand up is having a hypoglycemic episode and needs a veterinarian now. It is not a dog that skipped a meal and will perk up.

Why the Cup Lies

The same calorie target produces wildly different cup volumes depending on which bag you open. Kibble density runs from roughly 300 to 480 kcal per cup, and a cup of one food can weigh 105 grams while a cup of another weighs 120. Both scoops look identical in the bowl.

Grams sidestep the whole question. The food's kcal/kg divides straight into the target, the scoop stops mattering, and the same number works whether the kibble is small-breed discs or standard pieces.

When the Adult Ration Starts

Maturity is not one age. Toys finish between 9 and 12 months, Miniatures between 12 and 15, and Standards keep filling out until 18 to 24 months even after they hit full height at around 11 or 12. Switching a Standard to adult food the month it stops growing taller is premature by half a year.

The reason it matters is mineral density, not calories. AAFCO's growth profiles set calcium at a minimum of 1.0 percent on a dry-matter basis, with a separate large-breed growth profile capping it at 1.8 percent. Adult maintenance formulas need only about 0.5 percent. A Standard puppy that gets adult food at 10 months loses that margin during the last stretch of bone remodeling, and one switched too young in the other direction can overshoot the ceiling in the first six months, when the damage that shows up as elbow or hip disease is actually done.

For a Toy or a Miniature the transition is quieter. Both are essentially done growing by a year, so the move from puppy formula to adult formula can happen between 10 and 12 months without much risk either way.

What to Change This Week

Weigh one cup of your kibble on a kitchen scale and write the number on the bag. Then read the kcal/kg figure off the guaranteed analysis panel and divide your target by it. Those two numbers replace every chart on the internet, including this one.

Weigh the puppy weekly for the first six months rather than monthly. At the small end a single pound is 20 percent of a Toy, so a bathroom scale will not do; a kitchen scale or a baby scale will. The Puppy Weight Predictor shows whether the growth curve you are tracking lands where the breed should.

Then check the body, not the number. Part the coat behind the shoulder and feel for ribs under a flat palm. On a Poodle in full coat this is the only reliable check, because the curl sits exactly where the waist should dip. The Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator converts a body condition score into a target weight, and the Breed Calorie Calculator gives the breed-typical weights to compare against.

Water is part of the portion. Dry kibble is about 10 percent moisture, so a Poodle eating 44 grams of it drinks nearly all of its water separately. The Dog Water Intake Calculator gives the daily target, roughly an ounce per pound.

Three things are not portion questions. A puppy that stops gaining weight for two straight weeks. A Toy that goes weak between meals more than once. A Standard that vomits after eating or retches without producing anything. Those get a vet visit, and the bowl stays where it is until someone has looked at the dog.
