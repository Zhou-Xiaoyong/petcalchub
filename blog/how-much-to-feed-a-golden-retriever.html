How Much to Feed a Golden Retriever: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 21, 2026 · 8 min read

A 65 lb Golden Retriever on a 380 kcal kibble needs 3.7 cups a day, split into two meals of a little under two cups each. That is the entire answer, and it is wrong for most Goldens, because the bowl in your kitchen rarely holds only kibble.

Portion size is the part of feeding a dog owners control completely, and the part most often decided by eye. A heaped scoop, a splash of wet food, six treats in the driveway. Each one is small. Together they run to about 470 calories a day, which on a Golden is a pound of body weight every week.

Quick answer: A 55 lb female needs about 3.3 cups of a 380 kcal/cup food per day; a 65 lb male about 3.7; a 75 lb male about 4.1. Split the daily total into two meals. If your kibble is denser (430 kcal/cup) drop to 3.3 cups; if it is a light formula (340 kcal/cup) go up to 4.2. Get the calorie target from the Dog Calorie Calculator first, then divide by kcal per cup from your own bag.

The Bag's Chart Is a Range, and a Generous One

Every complete dog food sold in the U.S. carries an AAFCO nutritional adequacy statement and a calorie content figure, most often in kcal/kg. That number is not the one printed in the feeding table on the back. The table converts it to cups for you, then brackets the result into a band like "3 to 4 1/4 cups," and the top of that band is written for an intact, active dog of that weight. For a neutered Golden sleeping on a dog bed for nine hours, the top of the band is roughly 20 to 30 percent over requirement.

Two owners can each report "four cups a day" and be 140 kcal apart. A cup of large-breed kibble weighs between 85 and 120 g depending on kibble size and shape, so a cup of a big ring-shaped formula holds fewer calories than a cup of small dense kibble at the same kcal/kg. Weigh one cup of your food once, on a kitchen scale, and write the gram figure on the lid of the bin. That single number makes every later measurement repeatable.

If the label only gives kcal/kg, divide by 1,000 to get kcal per gram, then multiply by the weight of your cup. A food at 3,780 kcal/kg with a 100 g cup works out to 378 kcal/cup. That is the figure the charts below assume when they say 380.

Body fat carries roughly 3,500 kcal per pound, so 470 surplus calories a day is about a pound a week, or 50 lb a year if nothing changes. That is not hypothetical. It is the ordinary path from a fit two-year-old to a chunky seven-year-old, on a ration the owner would swear never changed.

The 10 percent rule gives you a ceiling: 141 kcal of non-kibble food on a 1,415 kcal target. Two tablespoons of peanut butter plus one dental chew already uses 270. Pull treats out of the kibble ration instead of adding them on top, and weigh the day's allowance into a container each morning.

When the Bowl Is Half Kibble, Half Canned

Mixed feeding is where portions go wrong fastest, because canned food is roughly a third as calorie-dense per gram as dry. A 13 oz can of adult dog food holds about 400 to 500 kcal, so 1.1 to 1.4 kcal per gram. Kibble sits at 3.5 to 4.2 kcal per gram. The same volume of the two foods is not the same meal.

Do the subtraction before you scoop. A 65 lb Golden on 1,415 kcal given half a can of food worth 220 kcal has 1,195 kcal left for kibble, which is 3.1 cups at 380 kcal/cup. Owners who add half a can on top of a full ration have added a quarter of a day's calories without touching the scoop. Canned food is useful for hydration and for palatability in an older dog, and it works fine as long as it comes out of the kibble side of the ledger.

The Feathering Hides the Ribs

Goldens are harder to score by eye than short-coated breeds, and the breed's own coat works against you. Long feathering on the chest, belly and tail blends the outline, so a dog at 7 out of 9 can still look broad rather than fat in a photograph. The summer clip does the opposite and makes a dog look thinner than it is for six weeks.

Use your hands. Run flat fingers along the rib cage: you should feel each rib under a thin layer of fat, the way you feel the bones on the back of your hand. From behind, the body should narrow behind the rib cage before the hips. From the side, the belly should tuck up. Two of those three failing means the portion is too large, whatever the chart says.

Meals per Day, and How the Portion Moves With Age

An eight-week-old Golden eats four small meals a day. By three months it is on three, and by six months two is enough. Two meals stays right for life, and it keeps any single stomach load smaller, which matters in a deep-chested breed where a large meal is one of the recognized risk factors for bloat.

Puppies need more energy per pound than adults, but the bigger issue in a Golden is the growth diet. Use a formula labeled for large-breed growth, where calcium is held between the AAFCO minimum of 1.0 percent and a maximum of 1.8 percent on a dry matter basis. Overfeeding calories and calcium in the first year raises the risk of the joint problems the breed is already prone to, and a Golden keeps filling out in chest and muscle until about two years even after reaching adult height near 12 to 15 months.

From about eight years, drop the calorie target to roughly 1.3 to 1.4 times resting energy, a 12 to 18 percent cut from the adult figure. Appetite does not usually fall with it, so the reduction has to be deliberate. Keep protein up while the volume comes down, since older dogs lose lean muscle faster.

What to Change Tomorrow Morning

Weigh one cup of your kibble and write the gram weight on the bin. Weigh the dog, put that weight into the calculator, and divide the daily total across two bowls. Then count everything else, including the chew, the topper and the training treats, against the 10 percent ceiling.

Give it three weeks before changing anything again, then let the scale rather than your eye decide. Move the portion in 10 percent steps, never 25. Two red lines are worth memorizing: a body condition score of 8 or 9 out of 9 means the weight-loss plan should be set with a veterinarian rather than improvised, and a Golden that loses 5 percent of its body weight in a month without a change in ration needs an exam, not a bigger bowl.
