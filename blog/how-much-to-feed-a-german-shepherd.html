How Much to Feed a German Shepherd: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 25, 2026 · 9 min read

A 75 lb German Shepherd eating a 360 kcal-per-cup food needs 4.4 cups a day. Give the same dog a 330 kcal large-breed formula and the bowl fills to 4.8 cups. A 380 kcal food pulls it back to 4.2. Same dog, same daily target, and six-tenths of a cup decided entirely by the bag in the pantry.

Then there is the number no bag prints. A nine-month-old male at 70 lb eats about 5.1 cups. More than he will eat at two years old, when he is heavier and finished growing. The biggest bowl of his life arrives before his first birthday and then shrinks.

Both facts come from the same arithmetic. The breed just makes each of them harder to see.

Quick answer: A neutered adult German Shepherd on a 360 kcal/cup large-breed kibble needs about 4.4 cups at 75 lb, 3.5 cups at 55 lb, and 5.0 cups at 90 lb. Weigh one cup before you trust any of those figures, because large-breed kibble runs 90 to 120 g per cup and that spread moves the day's ration by half a cup on its own. Settle the calorie target in the Dog Calorie Calculator first, then convert.

Why the Cup Count Wanders

Every complete dog food sold in the U.S. states energy density in kcal/kg, an AAFCO labeling rule. That figure is fixed by the recipe and does not move. The cup is the part that moves.

Kibble geometry decides how tightly a cup packs. Large-breed formulas are built from big rings and thick discs, so a level cup lands between 90 and 120 g. Adult large-breed foods also run leaner on calories, typically 330 to 380 kcal per cup, against 400 to 480 for small-breed formulas. Feed a Shepherd a bag meant for a toy breed and the cup count drops by a third, which reads as good news until you notice the dog is hungry at 3 p.m.

A level 110 g cup versus the same cup heaped to 150 g is 36 percent more food. Across a 4.4-cup ration the bowl gains about 575 kcal a day, a third of the day's energy again, delivered by a hand that thinks it is measuring. Weigh one cup on a kitchen scale this week and write the grams on the lid of the bin. That single number does more for portion accuracy than any feeding chart.

The Bag Chart Is Written for a Dog You Do Not Have

Feeding guides on the back of the bag assign a portion by adult body weight. The AKC standard puts German Shepherd males at 65 to 90 lb and females at 55 to 75. The German SV standard runs wider, 30 to 40 kg for males and 22 to 32 kg for females. That is 35 lb of legitimate adult range inside one breed, and 35 lb is about 350 kcal a day.

A chart printed for a range that wide cannot be right for a working-line female at 60 lb and a show-line male at 88 lb at the same time. It also cannot see the dog in front of you. Feed the current weight and adjust from body condition, not from a projected adult size.

Puppy charts are worse, because they assign volume by an adult weight the breeder predicted. A four-month Shepherd is halfway to a number nobody can forecast within 15 lb. Feed the puppy you weighed this morning, and check the Breed Calorie Calculator for this breed's typical adult spread.

Eight Weeks to Two Years

This breed grows for 18 to 24 months, which is longer than most of the portion charts assume. Energy needs per kilogram fall steadily after four months. Body weight keeps climbing. The two curves cross, and the crossing is what owners miss.

Read the 9-month and 24-month rows together. The adolescent takes 5.1 cups and the finished adult takes 4.6. An owner who keeps filling the same bowl through that transition adds roughly 190 kcal a day to a dog whose growth is ending, which is a pound every 18 days.

The other direction is worse. Pushing extra volume into a puppy to build size does not build bone. Keep the ribs easy to feel at every weigh-in and let the growth curve finish on its own schedule, because the puppy fed to the top of a bag chart is usually the one carrying too much weight through the months his joints are still forming.

One Bowl or Three

German Shepherds sit high on the list of breeds prone to gastric dilatation-volvulus. Lifetime risk across large breeds runs near 5.7 percent in the Glickman studies, and this breed and its first-degree relatives carry more than that. Per-meal volume is one of the few variables an owner actually controls.

Two findings from that work surprise people. A raised bowl increased risk rather than reducing it, and so did a single large meal each day. Feed three meals of 1.6 cups instead of one bowl of 4.8 and the stomach holds a third of the volume at a time.

Soaking the kibble helps a dog that bolts his food, since water adds volume without adding calories. Add warm water, let it sit five minutes, and the same 241 g per meal arrives as a slower, bulkier mouthful. Pair it with a slow feeder if he finishes in under two minutes, and keep the water bowl full: dry-fed dogs drink more, and the Dog Water Intake Calculator gives a daily target by body weight. Unproductive retching, a tight distended belly, drooling and pacing are not watch-and-wait signs. That dog goes to a clinic immediately.

At Nine and Older

The 1.6 multiplier comes down to about 1.2 for a senior Shepherd on short walks, and the bowls get smaller without the dog looking thinner. Four and a half cups becomes 3.6. That is the intended change, not a problem to correct with a topper.

Watch the shape rather than the number. This breed loses muscle along the spine before it loses fat over the ribs, so an old Shepherd can read as thin from above while carrying too much around the waist.

A Shepherd that stops finishing meals, or drops weight on a ration that should hold him, needs an exam rather than a different flavor. Loss of appetite in an older large breed is a clinical sign, and the calorie targets behind these volumes are worked out in the German Shepherd Calorie Calculator.

What to Change Tomorrow Morning

Weigh one cup of your actual kibble and write the grams on the bin. Put the measuring cup back in the drawer and use the scale from then on. Weigh the dog on the same scale, same time of day, every two weeks.

Treats get 10 percent of the day, about 158 kcal at the 75 lb row. A large dental chew runs 60 to 100 kcal, so one is a real deduction from dinner rather than a bonus after it.

Change the portion in 10 percent steps and give each step three weeks before you judge it. Three things do not belong to a feeding chart: a dog that vomits after most meals, a dog losing weight on a full ration, and a dog that cannot finish a walk he managed last month. Those are veterinary appointments. Everything else is a number in a bowl you can change tonight.
