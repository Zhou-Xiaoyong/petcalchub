How Much to Feed a Beagle: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 30, 2026 · 8 min read

One hundred and eight grams of kibble is a full meal for a 25 lb Beagle on a 3,200 kcal/kg formula. Move to the bag beside it on the shelf, at 4,000 kcal/kg, and the same meal is 87 grams. Same dog, same calories, different scoop.

A Beagle's daily ration is small enough that a cup is a blunt instrument, and the standard's weight cap leaves no room to absorb the error. Under 20 lb for the 13-inch dog, 20 to 30 for the taller one. Very few pet Beagles at a US clinic weigh in under those numbers, and the extra pounds arrive a few grams at a time.

Quick answer: Run RER = 70 × (kg)0.75 on the weight the dog should be, then multiply by 1.4 for a housedog with a daily walk or 1.6 for one getting a real hour of scent work. A 25 lb Beagle lands at 432 kcal resting, 605 or 692 to feed. Divide by the food's kcal per gram and you have 168 to 192 grams a day, in two bowls. One Target, Three Foods, Three Different Scoops

Every bag sold in the US prints a calorie statement, and it appears in one of two forms. The better labels give kcal/kg, a figure in the guaranteed analysis that never changes with scoop size. The rest give kcal per cup, which is a volume and therefore a moving target.

Take the 692 kcal target for a 25 lb Beagle at 1.6 and run it through three common adult formulas:

A 0.62-cup spread between the first bag and the third, on the same instruction to feed "about two cups." That gap is 176 kcal, which over a year is 18 lb of theoretical gain.

A Cup Is a Volume, and Kibble Size Fills It Differently

Even inside one density figure, the cup is unreliable. A cup of small-bite kibble packs to 115 or 125 grams. A cup of a large-biscuit formula sits at 85 to 95 grams because more of the cup is air. Same volume, and one holds nearly half again as much food as the other.

The fix takes fifteen seconds and costs nothing. Set the bowl on a kitchen scale, press tare, and scoop until the display reads the gram number instead of stopping when the cup looks full. Do it once, write the number on a sticky note inside the food bin, and the scoop stops mattering.

The error scales the wrong way. A ten percent scoop error on a 100 lb Labrador is 90 kcal against a 2,000 kcal day, which is noise. The same ten percent on a 25 lb Beagle is 69 kcal against 692, and 69 kcal happens to be the entire ten percent treat allowance. Small dogs do not get small mistakes, they get proportionally larger ones.

Eight Weeks to Fifteen Months

Puppies are not small adults on a bigger appetite. Growth sits on top of maintenance, and veterinary feeding guides handle it with a staged multiplier rather than one figure for the first year:

  • Under four months: 3.0 × RER. The ration looks absurd next to a seven-pound puppy. It is correct.
  • Four to six months: 2.5 × RER. The steep part of the growth curve is behind the dog.
  • Six months to about 80 percent of adult weight: 2.0 × RER.
  • Final stretch to maturity: 1.6 × RER, close enough to adult that the change is small.

A Beagle hits 80 percent of adult weight somewhere around eight or nine months and finishes between twelve and fifteen. Owners who switch to adult portions at six months because the dog looks grown are cutting the ration three months early, and the dog spends that time hungry with nothing to show for it.

Grams assume 3,600 kcal/kg. If your bag says 4,000, drop each figure by ten percent; at 3,200, add twelve. The meal counts follow the usual schedule: four meals until twelve weeks, three until six months, two after that.

Predicting the adult weight is the one piece of arithmetic worth doing early, because the whole chart depends on it. A medium breed at sixteen weeks weighs roughly half what it will weigh grown, so a 13 lb puppy at four months points to 26 lb, mid-range for the taller variety. One rule you can skip: AAFCO's large-breed growth profile covers dogs headed past 70 lb. A Beagle never gets near it, so the calcium limits written for Labrador puppies do not apply.

The Second Bowl You Do Not Fill

A Beagle finds food. That is not a character flaw, it is the job the breed was built for: nose at floor height, hours of patience, and a strong opinion about anything edible. Which means the kitchen floor is a food source, and the day's calories are not only the ones you scooped.

  • One slice of buttered toast off a plate: about 120 kcal
  • A quarter cup of cat kibble left down all day: about 130 kcal
  • One ounce of cheddar from a dropped block: about 110 kcal
  • A tablespoon of peanut butter from a licked knife: about 95 kcal

The cat bowl is the one that does real damage, because it is a daily habit rather than an accident. A quarter cup of cat kibble is denser than dog food and runs near 130 kcal. Left available for a year, that is 47,000 calories, or thirteen pounds of dog. A family with a cat and a Beagle is often running two feeding programs on one floor, and only one of them is measured.

The hunger is not evidence of underfeeding. A Beagle that has eaten 692 kcal and would happily eat 692 more is behaving exactly as the breed should. Feed to the number, not to the request, and put the cat's bowl somewhere the dog's shoulders will not fit.

After the Spay or Neuter

Gonadal hormones are part of the energy equation, and removing them lowers the requirement, commonly by 20 to 30 percent in the months that follow. A 25 lb Beagle eating 692 kcal at 1.6 that settles to 1.2 lands at 519. That is 173 kcal of ration the dog no longer needs, roughly half a cup, and nobody sends a letter about it.

Recalculate at the three-month mark rather than at the three-year mark. Owners who keep the same bowl through the surgery year usually report three or four pounds gained in six months, and then a plateau at the new set point.

What to Change This Week

Weigh the bowl, not the dog. Put the bowl on the scale, tare it, and scoop to the gram figure. A 25 lb Beagle at 692 kcal takes 96 grams twice a day on a 3,600 kcal/kg food, and the scale will hold that number while a cup will not.

Split it in two, twelve hours apart. One large meal a day is the fastest route to a dog that counter-surfs at five in the afternoon, and this breed keeps a precise clock. Lift the bowl after fifteen minutes whether or not it is empty.

Pay treats out of the cup rather than on top of it. Ten kibbles pulled from the weighed ration cost about 10 kcal, and a Beagle will work a nose-work game for those ten as hard as for any biscuit. When the treats come from a bag, keep the count honest: 69 kcal is the day's allowance, and a medium biscuit is 40.

Then weigh monthly on the same scale and write it down. One pound on a 25 lb dog is four percent of body weight, which is inside the range where a scoop error hides for a year. If a home scale is not practical, use the clinic's and run hands over the ribs weekly in between.

A Beagle that leaves food in the bowl is telling you something. This is a breed that has never walked away from a meal, so a skipped dinner, two days of picking, or vomiting within an hour of eating is a phone call to the vet rather than a portion adjustment.

Everything else is arithmetic you can do tonight with a scale, a bag, and the number on the label. Start with the gram figure, not the cup. The cup is what got the dog to 34 lb in the first place.
