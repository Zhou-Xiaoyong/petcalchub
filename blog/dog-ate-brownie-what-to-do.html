Dog Ate a Brownie? Here's Exactly What to Do

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published August 29, 2026 · 7 min read

A brownie feels like a harmless dessert — mostly flour, sugar, butter, and eggs, with "some" chocolate stirred in. That intuition is exactly what makes brownies one of the more deceptive chocolate incidents a dog owner will face. Brownies are almost never made from milk chocolate. They are built on cocoa powder and unsweetened baking chocolate, the two most theobromine-dense ingredients in any kitchen. On top of that, a brownie brings extras a candy bar never does: walnuts, espresso powder, a heavy fat load, and sometimes raisins or cannabis. Here is how to size up the real risk and what to do in the next hour.

Quick answer: One 2-inch homemade fudgy brownie carries roughly 60–120 mg of theobromine — about as much as a full milk chocolate bar and a half. That is a non-event for a 70 lb Labrador and a genuine emergency for a 5 lb Chihuahua. Weigh the situation with our Chocolate Toxicity Calculator, then call a poison hotline if you land above the mild band.

Why a Brownie Isn't the Same as a Candy Bar

Theobromine lives in cocoa solids, so concentration tracks how little the cocoa has been diluted with sugar, milk, and cocoa butter. Milk chocolate runs about 44 mg of theobromine per ounce. Unsweetened baking chocolate runs roughly 450 mg/oz, and cocoa powder about 735 mg/oz — roughly ten to seventeen times stronger by weight.

A brownie recipe does dilute that cocoa into a batter, which is why a brownie is not as concentrated per gram as raw cocoa powder. But recipes use a lot of it. A standard 9×13 pan typically contains three-quarters of a cup of cocoa powder, or four ounces of baking chocolate, or both. Cut into 24 squares, each square inherits a meaningful slice of a very strong starting dose. The dilution is real but modest; the assumption that "it's just a baked good" is what gets dogs into trouble.

How Much Theobromine Is in One Brownie?

The table below estimates theobromine per 2-inch square (about 45–55 g) for a 9×13 pan cut into 24 pieces. These are planning figures, not laboratory values, but they are accurate enough to decide whether you are watching your dog or driving to a clinic.

Squares to Reach Each Risk Band, by Dog Weight

Veterinarians grade chocolate cases in milligrams of theobromine per kilogram of body weight: mild signs from about 20 mg/kg, severe cardiac and neuromuscular signs from about 40 mg/kg, and a potentially fatal band from about 60 mg/kg. Using the common homemade fudgy brownie at 75 mg per square, here is how many squares it takes to cross each line:

Two things jump out. First, a single square puts a 5 lb dog past the mild threshold and close to severe — a toy breed that finishes one brownie is a phone call, not a wait-and-see. Second, a 70 lb dog would need most of the pan to reach the same place, which is exactly why the same incident can be trivial or critical depending on who ate it. The Ingredients That Aren't Chocolate

This is where brownies genuinely differ from a candy bar. Even when the theobromine dose looks survivable, the mix-ins can create a second, separate problem.

  • Walnuts and pecans. Not inherently toxic, but very high in fat and prone to mold. Moldy nuts can carry tremorgenic mycotoxins such as penitrem A, which cause tremors and seizures that look like severe chocolate poisoning. Large pieces can also lodge in a small dog's gut.
  • Macadamia nuts. Genuinely toxic to dogs. Doses near 2 g per kilogram of body weight can produce weakness, hind-limb ataxia, vomiting, tremors, and fever — and macadamia signs stack on top of theobromine signs.
  • Raisins, dried cherries, or trail-mix add-ins. Grapes and raisins can cause acute kidney injury in dogs, and no reliably safe dose has been established. Any raisin exposure deserves its own assessment with the Grape & Raisin Toxicity Calculator.
  • Espresso or instant coffee powder. Caffeine is a methylxanthine like theobromine and adds to the same total stimulant burden.
  • Butter and oil. A brownie is roughly a third fat by calories. A large fatty meal is a recognized trigger for acute pancreatitis, especially in Miniature Schnauzers and other predisposed dogs. Vomiting that starts a day later may be pancreatitis rather than theobromine.
  • "Keto" or sugar-free brownies. These may be sweetened with xylitol, which is far more acutely dangerous to dogs than the chocolate itself — it causes rapid hypoglycemia and, at higher doses, liver failure. Check the label and run the Xylitol Toxicity Calculator immediately.
  • Cannabis edibles. Brownies are the classic vehicle. THC toxicosis causes wobbling, dribbling urine, dilated pupils, and profound lethargy. Tell your veterinarian the truth; they are treating your dog, not filing a report, and the two toxins need different management.

What to Do in the First Hour

  1. Secure the pan and count what's missing. Estimate squares eaten, not "some." If a comparable square is left, put it on a kitchen scale — grams beat guesses.
  2. Identify the recipe. Boxed mix or homemade? Cocoa powder or baking chocolate? Nuts, raisins, coffee, sugar-free sweetener, cannabis? Keep the box or pull up the recipe.
  3. Calculate the dose. Enter chocolate type, weight eaten, and your dog's weight into the Chocolate Toxicity Calculator to get an mg/kg figure and risk band. For a cocoa-powder brownie, treating the cocoa fraction as the active ingredient is the conservative approach.
  4. Call a poison hotline with your numbers ready. ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center: (888) 426-4435. Pet Poison Helpline: (855) 764-7661. Both charge a consultation fee and both are staffed by veterinary toxicologists.
  5. Do not induce vomiting on your own. Home emesis risks aspiration pneumonia and is contraindicated in dogs that are already symptomatic or brachycephalic. Let the clinic decide — decontamination is most useful within the first one to two hours anyway.
  6. Watch for 24 to 72 hours. Theobromine has a half-life of roughly 17.5 hours in dogs, so signs can appear late and persist. Note the time of every symptom; a written timeline is genuinely useful to the veterinary team.

The Bottom Line

A brownie is a strong-chocolate event wearing the disguise of a mild one. The batter is built from the two most concentrated cocoa products available, so one homemade square can rival a whole milk chocolate bar, and small dogs can cross the severe threshold on a single piece. Do three things: work out the theobromine dose for your dog's actual weight, inventory the mix-ins for a second toxin or a pancreatitis-sized fat load, and call poison control with those numbers instead of waiting to see what happens. Dogs treated early almost always recover fully.
