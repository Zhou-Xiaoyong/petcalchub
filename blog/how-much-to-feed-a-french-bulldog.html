How Much to Feed a French Bulldog: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 23, 2026 · 9 min read

A 24 lb French Bulldog eating a 420 kcal-per-cup kibble needs 1.2 cups a day, split into two bowls of about 0.6 cup. Most owners scoop closer to two cups, because 1.2 cups of small kibble looks like a snack on a plate that wide.

The calorie target for this breed is settled arithmetic. Resting energy from body weight, multiplied by a factor near 1.2 for a dog whose airway limits hard exercise. What goes wrong is the conversion to volume. Kibble varies by 40 percent in calories per cup, and a scoop filled by eye varies by a third from the same cup filled level.

Both errors run the same direction.

Quick answer: Feed a neutered adult French Bulldog about 1.2 cups of a 420 kcal/cup small-breed kibble per day at 24 lb, 0.89 cup at 16 lb, and 1.35 cups at 28 lb. Split it into two meals and use a scale, not a scoop. Confirm the daily kcal for your own dog in the Dog Calorie Calculator first, because a flat-faced dog needs the 1.2 multiplier rather than the 1.6 a working breed gets.

Why a Cup Is the Weakest Link in the Bowl

Every complete dog food sold in the U.S. states its energy content in kcal/kg, an AAFCO labeling requirement. That number is fixed by the recipe. The cup is not. Kibble shape and pellet size change how tightly a cup packs, so one cup of large-breed rings weighs 85 to 120 g while a cup of small-breed pellets usually lands between 100 and 115 g.

Calorie density moves with it. Small-breed formulas commonly run 400 to 480 kcal per cup. Large-breed adult formulas sit nearer 330 to 380. Feed a Frenchie a large-breed food with the scoop you used on the last bag and the day's ration drops by a quarter, with nothing on the label to announce it.

Scoop technique costs more than formula choice. A level cup of small kibble weighs about 105 g. The same cup with a rounded top holds 140 to 150 g, which is 34 to 43 percent more food. On a 504 kcal target that is an extra 170 to 215 calories a day, delivered by a hand that believes it is measuring. Weigh one cup once and write the gram figure on the bin lid. If the bag gives only kcal/kg, divide by 1,000 and multiply by the cup weight: 4,200 kcal/kg with a 105 g cup is 441 kcal per cup.

The 16 lb row is where most adult females belong, and it is roughly half a cup less per day than their owners pour.

The heaped scoop supplies 50 of the surplus. The extras supply 164, and every one of them was a reasonable decision on its own. Body fat holds about 3,500 kcal per pound, so 214 calories a day is roughly 0.4 lb a week. A year of it is 22 lb, which on a 24 lb frame means a second French Bulldog assembled one rounded scoop at a time.

The 10 percent rule gives the ceiling: 50 kcal of anything that is not the complete diet on a 504 kcal day. One tablespoon of peanut butter uses nearly twice that. Pull treats out of the kibble ration rather than stacking them on top, and the arithmetic holds without the dog going hungry.

Puppies: Volume From Eight Weeks to a Year

A Frenchie puppy reaches adult weight around 11 to 12 months, earlier than a retriever, so the growth diet has a shorter runway. Feed a formula labeled for growth or for all life stages, and skip large-breed puppy food built around calcium limits this breed does not need.

Four meals at eight weeks, three by three months, two by six. The reason is mechanical as much as metabolic. A puppy that bolts a large bowl swallows air with the kibble, and a flat-faced puppy tolerates swallowed air badly. Smaller, more frequent meals cut the gulping and most of the gas that goes with it.

Weigh the puppy weekly for six months and move the portion in 10 percent steps. Do not hold a growing Frenchie back to keep it small. Restricting growth costs lean mass and buys nothing that lasts. The calorie targets behind these volumes are worked out in the French Bulldog Calorie Calculator.

When the Goal Is Weight Loss

Feed the dog you want, not the dog you have. Take an ideal weight from body condition, run resting energy at 1.0× on that number, and treat the result as the day's ceiling. A 30 lb Frenchie whose ideal is 24 lb eats the 504 kcal row, not the 625 kcal row its current weight produces.

Aim for 1 to 2 percent of body weight per week. On a 24 lb dog that is 0.24 to 0.48 lb, slower than most owners expect and slow enough to keep muscle on a frame that cannot afford to lose it. Weigh at the clinic or on a baby scale. A bathroom scale read while you hold the dog carries about half a pound of error, which is two weeks of progress at this size.

The Topper Spiral

A Frenchie that walks away from plain kibble gets a spoon of wet food, then warm water, then a rotation of flavors. Within a month the dog refuses anything unadorned and the topper has added 60 to 120 calories to a 504 kcal day.

Wet food is roughly a third as calorie-dense per gram as dry, so a 3 oz can holds about 90 kcal. That is not the problem. The problem is that it goes on top instead of coming out of the kibble. Take 90 kcal off the dry side, a little over a fifth of a cup at 420 kcal per cup, and the total holds while the bowl still looks generous.

Set the bowl down for ten minutes and lift it. The free-fed Frenchie is the one most likely to reach four years old carrying 8 lb of extra fat.

What to Change Tomorrow Morning

Weigh a cup of your kibble, write the grams on the bin, and put the measuring cup away. From then on the kitchen scale is the scoop. Weigh the dog, put both numbers into the calculator, and split the daily total across two bowls.

Treats come out of the ration rather than on top of it. Ten percent of 504 kcal is 50, and half a medium dental chew uses most of that budget. A chew is a deduction from dinner, not a bonus after it.

Three things belong to a veterinarian rather than a calculator. A body condition score of 8 or 9 out of 9 means the weight-loss plan needs supervision, because a brachycephalic dog on a steep deficit loses lean mass first. A Frenchie that refuses food for 24 hours, or vomits after two meals in a row, needs an exam. Labored breathing at rest, blue gums, or a collapse after a short walk is an airway emergency, whatever the bowl says.
