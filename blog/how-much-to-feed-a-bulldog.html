How Much to Feed a Bulldog: Portions by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

A 50 lb Bulldog gets roughly 270 grams of kibble a day. That is 9.5 oz, about 30 percent of a 2 lb bag, and it vanishes into a wide bowl. It also fits inside a 12 oz coffee mug, which is where most of this goes wrong.

The calorie side is settled. The argument in most Bulldog households is about volume, because 270 g looks like a starvation ration and two mugs looks like a normal breakfast. Both can be true.

Quick answer: A neutered 50 lb Bulldog needs about 950 kcal a day, which is 2.7 cups at 350 kcal per cup, or 270 g, split into two 135 g meals. At 400 kcal per cup it is 2.4 cups. At 300 it is 3.2. Get the calorie target from the Dog Calorie Calculator first, then convert it to grams on a scale once.

Why the Volume Looks Wrong

Adult kibble runs 330 to 400 kcal per cup, so the 946 kcal a Dog Calorie Calculator run returns lands between 2.4 and 2.9 cups. That is a real bowl of food. The weight is what surprises people, because kibble is mostly air between pieces and 270 g of it fills about 2.7 cups.

Then the mug arrives. A standard 12 oz mug holds 1.5 cups, so two mugs is 3 cups. At 350 kcal per cup that is 1,050 kcal, or about 104 more than the dog needs, and a Bulldog will finish it in four minutes. Run that daily and the dog gains a pound every five weeks.

The grams column assumes 3.5 kcal per gram, which is what a 350 kcal cup weighs at 100 g. Check that assumption for your own bag before you trust the scale.

A Cup Is a Volume, Not a Weight

Two foods that both declare 350 kcal per cup can weigh very different amounts. Large, puffy kibble packs about 85 g into a cup. Dense small kibble packs 115 g into the same cup. Do that math on a 2.7-cup ration and you get 230 g against 310 g, a third more calories from two scoops that look identical.

The label settles it. Adult food prints kcal per kilogram next to the guaranteed analysis, and dividing by 1,000 gives kcal per gram. A 3,500 kcal/kg food is 3.5 kcal per gram, so 946 kcal is 270 g. At 3,200 it is 296 g; at 3,800 it is 249 g.

Weigh that once. Then either use the scale every day or pour 270 g into your scoop and mark the line with a marker. Either is fine. Eyeballing it is not.

Two Meals of 135 Grams

Split the day down the middle, roughly 8:00 and 6:00. A Bulldog should take 8 to 12 minutes over a meal. If the bowl is empty in under three, the food is going down whole and the dog is swallowing air with it, which is where a lot of the gas comes from.

A wide shallow bowl or a slow-feeder insert fixes the speed problem in a way that a smaller portion never will. Same food, same 135 g, four times as long to eat.

Do not leave the bowl down. A Bulldog that grazes eats more than one on two sittings, and free-feeding makes tomorrow's weight meaningless as a measurement.

Puppies From Eight Weeks

Four meals a day at eight weeks, three by twelve weeks, two by six months. Puppy food runs 2.0 to 3.0 times resting energy, and that figure drops every month, so recalculate at each vet visit instead of guessing at the bag.

One detail that surprises owners: at a 50 lb adult weight, a Bulldog is a medium breed under AAFCO nutrient rules, not a large breed. The calcium caps and slow-growth formulas written for Labs and Great Danes are not required here. An all-life-stages diet fed to a lean puppy is fine, and keeping the puppy lean matters more than which bag you choose.

Bulldog puppies look chunky at eight weeks and owners read that as healthy. Feel the ribs instead. A puppy at 6 of 9 is being set up for a stifle problem before its first birthday.

Filling the Bowl Without Filling the Dog

A Bulldog on 946 kcal acts hungry, and a hungry Bulldog is loud. Add volume that carries almost no calories and the bowl looks normal without the day going over.

Keep it plain and keep it inside the treat budget. A half cup of beans plus a half cup of pumpkin is 60 kcal, which leaves 35 of the 95 kcal allowance. Seasoned vegetable mixes are a different food: onion and garlic powder are toxic to dogs, and salt is not doing the kidneys any favors.

Reading the Bag vs Reading the Dog

Bag charts are written for an intact, active dog of that weight. A 50 lb row that recommends 3 to 3.5 cups is telling you 1,050 to 1,225 kcal. A neutered Bulldog wants 946, so the chart runs 10 to 30 percent high before you add a single treat.

Use the bag for kcal per cup and for nothing else. That is the one number on the label that describes the food rather than an imaginary dog. Portion size comes from the calculator, and the calculator needs a target weight you decided on, not the number the scale happened to show this morning.

If your dog needs to lose weight, get the target from the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator and feed to that number at a 1.0 factor. Water intake is worth a look too, since a dog drinking far more than its daily water target is a different kind of question.

What to Change This Week

Weigh 270 g into your usual scoop and look at where the line falls. Most owners find their scoop holds 20 to 30 percent more than they assumed, which is the entire weight problem in one measurement.

Treats get 95 kcal, the 10 percent every calculator uses. A baby carrot is 3 kcal, so the budget is 31 of them. A medium biscuit is about 40, which is 42 percent of the day's allowance for one bite. A level tablespoon of peanut butter is 95 and uses all of it.

Weigh the dog every two weeks, same scale, same time of day, and cut the portion 10 percent at a time with three weeks between changes. Expect a quarter to a half pound a week on this ration. Faster than that is muscle coming off a dog that cannot spare it.

Four things stop being portion questions. Vomiting after most meals. Refusing food for a full day, which Bulldogs almost never do. Weight gain on a measured 946 kcal. And a dog that cannot finish a walk it managed last month. Those are exams.

Write the grams on the lid of the bin tonight. The bowl is the part you control, and it is a measuring problem rather than a willpower problem.
