Chocolate Toxicity in mg/kg: The Number Vets Actually Use

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published August 22, 2026 · 7 min read

"How much chocolate is too much?" is the wrong question for a dog. It mixes two different things — how much chocolate (a vague product) and too much for whom (a dog whose size you have not yet used). Veterinarians don't answer the question that way. They ask for a single number expressed in milligrams of theobromine per kilogram of your dog's body weight, abbreviated mg/kg. That single ratio is what decides whether a dog is fine, needs monitoring, or needs the ER right now. This article explains why that unit became universal, how to read it, and how to do the math before you call.

Quick answer: mg/kg stands for milligrams of theobromine per kilogram of dog body weight. Below 20 mg/kg is generally safe, ~40 mg/kg is severe, and ~60 mg/kg is the potentially-fatal threshold. The right number depends on chocolate type, amount eaten, and your dog's weight — see our Chocolate Toxicity Calculator to get yours in seconds.

Why Vets Use mg/kg Instead of "Ounces of Chocolate"

Theobromine — the toxic compound in chocolate — has the same molecular effect on every dog it reaches, but the dose that produces the effect depends entirely on the dog's size. A 5 lb puppy and a 100 lb Great Dane cannot be compared by ounces of the same chocolate bar; the puppy gets a far higher concentration in its bloodstream from a much smaller absolute amount. Toxicology solves this exact problem by normalizing dose to body weight. Milligrams per kilogram gives you a single, comparable number that holds whether the dog is a Yorkie or a Mastiff.

Every clinical threshold in veterinary toxicology is expressed in mg/kg for this reason. The Merck Veterinary Manual, ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center published ranges, peer-reviewed toxicology papers, and veterinary school teaching all speak the same language: not "this much chocolate," but "this many mg of theobromine per kg of dog." When you call a poison hotline with your dog's age, weight, and what they ate, the specialist immediately converts your story into mg/kg. That number is the only thing that drives the recommendation.

The Formula in Plain English

The math has three inputs and one output. Inputs: theobromine concentration in the product (mg per ounce, or mg per gram), amount of product ingested (ounces or grams), and the dog's body weight (kilograms). The output is mg/kg. Worked example with a 30 lb dog that ate 1.5 oz of 70% dark chocolate:

Step Calculation Example
1. Total theobromine ingestedmg per unit × units eaten210 mg/oz × 1.5 oz = 315 mg
2. Convert dog weight to kglbs ÷ 2.205 (or kg direct)30 lb ÷ 2.205 = 13.6 kg
3. mg/kg dosetotal theobromine ÷ dog weight in kg315 ÷ 13.6 = 23.2 mg/kg

That final number — 23.2 mg/kg in the worked example — is the line you read across to the risk bands. It sits just above the 20 mg/kg mild-symptom threshold, meaning the dog's owner should call the vet and watch for vomiting or restlessness, but the dog is unlikely to seize. The math takes 60 seconds and converts panic into a plan.

Reading the Chocolate Label the Right Way

The label almost never prints "mg theobromine per ounce." It prints "percent cacao" and a net weight in grams. To get the number you need, two conversions must happen in your head. First, percent cacao roughly tracks theobromine concentration: a 70% cacao bar is far more concentrated than a 30% cacao bar, with white chocolate near 0%. Second, you have to translate percent into mg/g using a published theobromine concentration table, then multiply by the grams of product eaten. Standard reference values are around 1.5 mg/g for milk chocolate, 6 mg/g for semi-sweet, 7-10 mg/g for dark chocolate, and 15-25 mg/g for unsweetened baking chocolate and cocoa powder.

There is a useful shortcut: for any chocolate bar, you can approximate theobromine content at about 1.5% of the bar's weight for milk, 2% for dark, and 3% for unsweetened. So a 100 g bar of dark chocolate contains roughly 2,000 mg of theobromine. This shortcut is good enough for triage but not for a hospital grade dose calculation; for that, always use a published concentration table. Our Chocolate Toxicity Calculator uses the conservative end of the published range so it errs toward overestimating the dose — which is the safe direction to be wrong.

The Three Clinical Thresholds

Once the dose is in mg/kg, you read it against the three clinical bands every veterinary toxicology textbook uses. Below ~20 mg/kg, dogs rarely develop true theobromine poisoning; they may have a touch of GI upset from the fat and sugar but the methylxanthine effect has not started. Around ~20-40 mg/kg, mild clinical signs appear — vomiting, diarrhea, restlessness, increased thirst. Around ~40-60 mg/kg, severe effects begin: cardiac arrhythmias, muscle tremors, agitation, elevated body temperature. At and above ~60 mg/kg, the dog is in danger zone territory where seizures and fatal arrhythmias become real risks without aggressive care.

Dose (mg/kg) Clinical picture Action
< 20Generally safe; possible mild GI upsetMonitor at home; call vet if symptoms appear
20–40Mild signs: vomiting, diarrhea, restlessnessCall vet; possible clinic visit
40–60Severe: cardiac arrhythmias, tremors, hyperthermiaVeterinary care needed; possible hospitalization
≥ 60Potentially fatal: seizures, collapseEmergency veterinary care immediately

These numbers are not cliffs — dogs vary by age, breed, individual sensitivity, and pre-existing heart or kidney conditions. They are decision shortcuts. A 25 mg/kg dose in a healthy adult dog is far safer than the same 25 mg/kg in a puppy with a heart murmur, even though the math is identical. That is one reason poison control always pairs the dose with a phone conversation.

A Worked Example You Can Reuse

Take a 40 lb dog that ate half a 12 oz bag of semi-sweet chocolate chips. At roughly 175 mg theobromine per ounce, half a bag is 6 oz × 175 = 1,050 mg total. A 40 lb dog is 18.1 kg. Divide: 1,050 ÷ 18.1 = 58 mg/kg — just under the 60 mg/kg fatal-threshold band, a clear trip-to-the-emergency-vet scenario. A kitchen scale and a calculator reach that number in under two minutes.

Now flip the numbers. Same 12 oz bag, but the dog is a 90 lb Lab: 1,050 mg ÷ 40.8 kg = 25.7 mg/kg — into the mild-signs band, where you call the vet, watch for vomiting, and likely don't need to rush in. Identical chocolate ingestion, identical product, but the 40 lb dog faces an emergency and the 90 lb dog faces a long afternoon. That is the entire reason mg/kg is the universal unit.

Why the Math Beats the Gut

Owners consistently misjudge the risk of any chocolate incident that does not involve obvious extremes — a small bar feels tiny and a big dog feels huge, so people default to "it'll probably be fine." The mg/kg calculation removes that bias by giving both dogs the same yardstick. After you run the numbers once, you stop thinking in ounces and start thinking in bands. That framework also travels well to other household toxins: xylitol and grapes both use mg/kg-style dose reasoning.

The Bottom Line

mg/kg is the single number a poison control specialist asks for because it is the only one that captures the whole problem at once. It rolls chocolate type, amount eaten, and the dog's weight into one ratio that maps directly to the clinical bands every vet is trained on. Once you understand the unit and the math, you stop guessing — you read off a band and act on it. The dose is the answer; run it before you make any other decision.
