Dog Feeding Chart by Weight and Age

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published September 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Feeding charts are useful in exactly one way: they give you a starting number that is far better than a guess. They are unreliable in another way that owners rarely hear about — printed charts are built on average energy needs, and real dogs scatter around that average by 20% or more. The trick is to use a chart as a first estimate, then let the dog's own body condition and the food's calorie density move you to the right number. Below are two charts worth keeping: calories and grams by body weight for adult dogs, and meals per day by age.

Quick answer: A typical neutered adult dog needs roughly 1.6 × its resting energy requirement, where RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. That is about 210 kcal a day at 5 lb and 1,960 kcal at 100 lb. Puppies need two to three times that relative to their weight. Convert the calories to grams using your food's kcal/kg, and confirm the result with a body condition check.

How to Read a Feeding Chart Without Getting It Wrong

Every chart here is expressed in calories first and grams second, and that order matters. Calories are the actual requirement; grams are just the volume of a particular food that delivers them. A chart that says "feed 2 cups" without telling you the food's energy density is an instruction only valid for one specific product on one specific day.

So use each row like this: find your dog's weight in the first column, read the calorie range, then divide by your food's calorie density to get grams. The Dog Calorie Calculator does the first part in seconds if you would rather not run the exponent by hand, and our guide to calculating portions by calorie covers the second part in detail.

The grams column assumes a typical maintenance kibble at 3,500 kcal/kg (3.5 kcal per gram). Because energy needs scale with body weight raised to the 0.75 power rather than linearly, the numbers grow more slowly than the weight column: a dog five times heavier than a Chihuahua does not eat five times as much. That is why a 100 lb dog eats about nine times the calories of a 5 lb dog, not twenty times.

Feeding Chart by Age: Puppies, Adults, and Seniors

Age changes the multiplier more than weight does. A growing puppy is building bone, muscle, and organ tissue, so it needs a large energy surplus relative to its size. A senior dog has usually slowed down and lost lean mass, so it needs less. The table below gives the factor to apply to RER at each stage.

The puppy factors deserve a note. Growth-energy requirements are not a smooth curve, and different clinical references split the bands slightly differently — some use 3.0 × RER until four months and 2.0 × RER afterwards, with no intermediate step. The 2.5 factor for the three-to-six-month window is a reasonable blend that accounts for the explosive growth spurt around four months, but it is a middle estimate rather than a hard rule. If your puppy looks thin or heavy against the growth curve, adjust on body condition rather than on the number in the table.

Breed Size Changes the Chart Again

Two puppies of identical weight at eight weeks can have very different futures, and that changes how you feed them. A Chihuahua is essentially fully grown at ten months; a Great Dane is still adding height and bone at eighteen to twenty-four months. The Breed Calorie Calculator builds those size tendencies into the estimate so you are not feeding a giant breed puppy as though it were a toy breed that happens to be large today.

The safety point here matters more than the calorie count. Large and giant breed puppies should be kept lean and should never be free-fed. Overfeeding energy — and, in large breeds, excess calcium — accelerates growth rate and raises the risk of developmental orthopedic disease: hip dysplasia, osteochondrosis, and other joint problems that can follow the dog for life. A large breed puppy that is slightly lean on a growth curve is in a better position than one that is slightly heavy. Because of that, large breed puppies should be fed a formula formulated for large breed growth, which controls calcium and energy density, rather than a generic puppy food.

Small and toy breed puppies have the opposite pattern: very high energy needs per pound, a small stomach, and a real risk of hypoglycemia if they skip meals. That is why the chart's four-meals-a-day row exists.

The Chart Is a Starting Point, Not the Answer

Once the chart gives you a number, the dog is the referee. Body condition scoring is the check, and it takes about thirty seconds: stand over the dog and look for a visible waist behind the ribs, run your hands along the ribcage — you should feel ribs easily under a thin fat layer, without seeing them — and feel for a slight tuck where the abdomen rises toward the hind legs. A score in the middle of the nine-point scale is the target; the Pet BMI & Ideal Weight Calculator turns those observations into a score and an ideal weight range if you want a number rather than a description.

Run that check every two to four weeks. If the ribs are getting harder to feel, subtract about 10% of the calories and recheck in two weeks. If the dog looks thin, add 10%. Small, deliberate adjustments with a two-week observation window beat any dramatic change, because a dog's weight responds slowly and it is easy to overshoot in either direction.

Adjusting the Chart for the Real World

Several factors routinely push a dog away from the table's default. Neutering lowers energy needs by roughly 20 to 30% in many dogs, which is why the adult factor here assumes a neutered animal; an intact dog of the same size usually needs more. Working and sporting dogs can run at 2 to 5 times RER during heavy training, and needs shift with the season, since cold weather increases the energy spent on thermoregulation. Ageing is gradual — a nine-year-old Labrador that was a normal 1.6 may now be closer to 1.4.

Treats belong inside the total, not on top of it; the widely used ceiling is 10% of daily calories, with balanced food supplying the rest so the diet stays complete. And if the plan is weight loss, never simply cut a chart number in half: go to the RER of the ideal weight and work from there, with veterinary guidance for anything beyond a modest reduction. The same is true of water, which changes alongside food intake — the Dog Water Intake Calculator gives a daily target by weight so you can tell whether your dog is drinking enough.

The Bottom Line

A feeding chart is a fast way to get a defensible starting number, and a poor way to finish the job. Read it in calories, convert to grams using your food's kcal/kg, and then let the dog settle the question: body condition, rechecked every few weeks, is the only measure that reflects the individual animal in front of you. Puppies need multiples of an adult's relative energy, large breeds need to be kept lean while they grow, and seniors need less than they did. Start with the table, adjust by 10%, and re-measure.
