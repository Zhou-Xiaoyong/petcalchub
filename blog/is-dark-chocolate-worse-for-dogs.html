Is Dark Chocolate Worse for Dogs? Theobromine by Chocolate Type

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published August 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Yes — and the gap is much wider than most owners assume. When a veterinary team asks "what kind of chocolate was it?", they are not making conversation. That single answer can move the same physical amount of chocolate from a harmless nibble to a life-threatening dose. The reason comes down to one ingredient that varies enormously between products: cocoa solids, and the theobromine locked inside them.

Quick answer: Ounce for ounce, dark chocolate carries roughly five times the theobromine of milk chocolate, and unsweetened baking chocolate carries about ten times. White chocolate has almost none. Identify the type first, then run the amount and your dog's weight through the Chocolate Toxicity Calculator.

What "% Cacao" Actually Tells You

Theobromine is a methylxanthine produced by the cacao bean. It lives in the non-fat cocoa solids — the dark, bitter component — and essentially not at all in cocoa butter, sugar, or milk. So the percentage printed on a chocolate bar is a surprisingly good proxy for canine risk. A 70% bar means 70% of its mass comes from cacao-derived ingredients; the rest is sugar and additions. A milk chocolate bar might be 10-15% cocoa solids, with the rest sugar, milk powder, and cocoa butter.

This is why "dark chocolate" is not one thing. A 55% semi-sweet bar and a 90% single-origin bar are different animals from a toxicology standpoint, and the calculator treats them differently. Dogs also cannot clear theobromine efficiently: its half-life in dogs is roughly 17.5 hours against about 7 hours in humans, so the compound accumulates while the animal's body slowly works through it.

Theobromine by Chocolate Type

Here are typical theobromine concentrations per ounce (28.35 g) of product, with milk chocolate used as the reference point:

Product Theobromine (mg/oz) vs milk chocolate
White chocolate~80.2×
Milk chocolate~441×
Semi-sweet chips~1754×
Dark chocolate, 60-70%~210~5×
Dark chocolate, 85%+~300~7×
Unsweetened baking chocolate~450~10×
Cocoa powder~735~17×

Brand and bean origin shift these numbers somewhat, but the ordering never changes: the darker and more bitter the product, the denser the theobromine.

One Ounce, One 30 lb Dog

Concentration only becomes meaningful once you divide it by a dog. Hold the amount and the animal constant — one ounce eaten by a 30 lb (13.6 kg) dog — and watch the type do all the work:

One ounce of… Dose (mg/kg) What it means
White chocolate~0.6Not a theobromine concern
Milk chocolate~3.2Well below mild threshold
Semi-sweet chips~12.9Approaching mild signs
Dark 60-70%~15.4Borderline — call for advice
Dark 85%+~22.0Mild signs expected
Baking chocolate~33.1Nearing cardiac band
Cocoa powder~54.0Approaching life-threatening

Same dog, same ounce, and the dose spans from a rounding error to near the 60 mg/kg danger band. That spread is the entire answer to "is dark chocolate worse". For smaller dogs the whole column shifts upward — a point we cover in detail in Chocolate Toxicity Calculator by Weight.

Where White Chocolate Fits

White chocolate contains cocoa butter but virtually no cocoa solids, so its theobromine content is trivially small. A dog would need to eat an implausible quantity to reach a toxic methylxanthine dose. That does not make it a treat: white chocolate is close to pure fat and sugar, and a large fatty ingestion can trigger vomiting, diarrhea, or pancreatitis, which is its own emergency in predisposed breeds like Miniature Schnauzers. The risk is real; it is simply a different mechanism.

Products That Hide More Cocoa Than You Think

The dangerous cases in practice are rarely a milk chocolate bar. They are the concentrated, unglamorous items sitting in a pantry:

  • Cocoa powder. The single most concentrated common household source. One tablespoon (~0.2 oz) holds roughly 150 mg of theobromine.
  • Cacao nibs and "raw cacao" health products. Marketed as wholesome, these are essentially crushed cocoa solids and sit in the same range as baking chocolate.
  • Chocolate-covered espresso beans. A double stimulant load — dark chocolate plus a meaningful caffeine dose from the bean itself.
  • Cocoa bean shell mulch. Garden mulch made from cocoa hulls has been reported to contain anywhere from about 50 to 850 mg of theobromine per ounce, and dogs are attracted to the smell. Treat any suspected mulch ingestion as a real exposure.
  • Baking supplies and brownie batter. An unbaked tray combines high cocoa density with an easy access height and, if the recipe is a sugar-free version, a possible xylitol problem on top.

The pattern is consistent: the products people do not think of as "candy" are usually the ones with the highest cocoa density.

Caffeine: The Second Stimulant

Chocolate contains caffeine as well as theobromine, and caffeine follows the same cocoa gradient. Milk chocolate holds roughly 6 mg of caffeine per ounce, dark chocolate and baking chocolate around 23 mg per ounce, and cocoa powder about 65 mg per ounce. Caffeine is a stronger stimulant on a per-milligram basis but is present in much smaller quantities, so theobromine remains the primary driver of clinical signs. Standard toxicity calculations account for total methylxanthines by using theobromine thresholds — but it is one more reason dark products punch harder than the theobromine figure alone suggests.

If You Are Not Sure Which Type It Was

When the wrapper is gone and nobody saw the incident, assume the worse category and calculate from there — it is far better to over-triage than to under-react. Search the bin for packaging, check what was in the house, and photograph anything you find. If the chocolate contained other ingredients, they may matter independently: chocolate-covered raisins raise the kidney-failure question addressed by our Grape & Raisin Toxicity Calculator, and any sugar-free or "no added sugar" chocolate needs a xylitol check with the Xylitol Toxicity Calculator before anything else.

The Bottom Line

Dark chocolate is worse for dogs because cocoa solids carry theobromine, and darker products are mostly cocoa solids. The spread from white chocolate to cocoa powder is close to ninety-fold by weight, which means the type of chocolate frequently matters more than the amount. Find out what your dog actually ate, calculate the mg/kg dose against its body weight, and call your veterinarian or a poison control center if the number lands anywhere near 20 mg/kg.
