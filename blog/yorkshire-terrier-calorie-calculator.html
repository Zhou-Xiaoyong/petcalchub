Yorkshire Terrier Calorie Calculator: Daily Food Needs

By the PetCalcHub Editorial Team · Published October 31, 2026 · 8 min read

A six-pound Yorkshire Terrier eats about 47 grams of kibble a day. Split across two meals that is 24 grams, roughly two level tablespoons of small-breed food, and a treat allowance of 18 calories.

Eighteen calories is one and a half small biscuits. It also decides whether this dog stays near six pounds or drifts to nine, because on a frame this small every value gets compressed. Two pounds is a third of the animal.

The breed standard is unusually blunt about the ceiling: weight must not exceed seven pounds. No floor, no range, no allowance for a big-boned line. That one sentence hands you the target the calculator needs.

Quick answer: Run RER = 70 × (kg)0.75, then multiply. A 6 lb (2.7 kg) Yorkie rests at 148 kcal. At 1.2 for a neutered apartment dog that is 178. At 1.4 with two real walks, 207. At 1.6 for a dog covering miles, 237. At 1.0 on the goal weight, 148. Enter the weight into the Dog Calorie Calculator and it returns the cups and grams along with the kcal.

A Ceiling, Not a Range

The Beagle standard gives you two weight bands. The Labrador standard gives you a fifteen-pound window and leaves the choice to you. The Yorkshire Terrier standard gives you a number and a direction, and it has read that way since the breed was formalized in the 1880s, when these dogs were small enough to tuck into a mill worker's coat.

Read the ceiling as a target and the arithmetic gets interesting. A seven-pound Yorkie is 3.2 kg. A nine-pound one is 4.1 kg. Those two dogs are 29 percent apart, the same gap as a 70 lb Labrador and a 90 lb one. Nobody calls a 90 lb Lab basically fine. Plenty of people call a nine-pound Yorkie normal, because it is standing next to a ten-pound one.

Twenty surplus calories a day is 7,300 in a year, or two pounds of fat. No single meal did it.

The 1.0 column runs on the weight the dog should be, not the weight on the scale this morning. An eleven-pound Yorkie holding steady at 1.4 needs 327 kcal. Fed at a seven-pound goal weight on 1.2, the target is 200. That 127-calorie gap is 39 percent of the current ration, and it has to come off in steps of about 10 percent every two to three weeks.

What the 0.75 Exponent Does Down Here

Energy need scales with weight to the power 0.75, so small animals burn more per pound. A four-pound Yorkie at 1.4 burns about 38 kcal per pound of body weight. A twelve-pound one burns 29. A seventy-pound Labrador burns 19. That also makes the factor look weaker than it is. Going from 1.2 to 1.4 adds 29 calories to a six-pound dog and 187 to a Labrador, but 29 is 14 percent of this dog's day. The curve across small breeds is worked through in calorie needs for small breed dogs.

The Factor This Breed Actually Earns

A Yorkshire Terrier was bred to kill rats in the mills of northern England, and the working version of that dog was wiry and relentless. The dog on your sofa is a different animal with the same name. Two fifteen-minute walks plus a patrol of the backyard is a 1.4 day at best. Add neutering, which drops maintenance 20 to 30 percent, and 1.2 is the honest number.

Trotting is the qualifier. A Yorkie covering two miles at a brisk pace four or five times a week earns 1.6. One on a retractable leash that stops to sniff every eight feet has not.

The whole ladder spans 133 calories, from 167 at 1.0 to 300 at 1.8. That is a third of a cup of food, and it is also the entire distance between a lean terrier and one the vet brings up at every annual visit.

No Undercoat, and Why That Changes the Number

This breed has hair, not fur. One layer, no undercoat, growing continuously the way human hair does. A Labrador's double coat traps a layer of still air against the skin. A Yorkie's coat lies flat, parts along the spine, and lets wind straight through to the skin.

That matters in February. Shivering is muscle work, and muscle work burns calories. A clipped Yorkie shivering through a Michigan winter can spend 10 to 15 percent more energy on thermoregulation than the same dog in July, which is 20 to 30 calories a day on a six-pound frame. Run the winter at 1.4 instead of 1.2 and the calculation lands where the dog already is. A floor-length show coat insulates nothing like a seven-blade pet clip, and a sweater costs less than the extra kibble.

When the Calculator Is Right and the Dog Is Not

Portosystemic shunt is the first thing to rule out, and the Yorkshire Terrier is one of the breeds where congenital shunts turn up most often. A shunt routes portal blood around the liver, so the liver never reaches full size and never clears ammonia. The dog eats, stays thin, and looks dull or unsteady thirty to ninety minutes after a meal. A few press their head into a wall. Bile acids drawn before and two hours after eating are the screen, and a single resting value misses a meaningful share of cases. A shunt dog on a hepatic formula is eating a different food at a different protein level, so 1.4 is a starting point there, not an answer.

Tracheal collapse makes a honk, not a bark, usually when the dog is excited or pulling. The cartilage rings flatten and the tube narrows, graded I through IV on fluoroscopy. Every pound on a three-kilogram frame adds load to a structure that is already softening. A harness instead of a collar is not a comfort upgrade; on a Grade II dog it removes the pressure that starts the cough.

Retained deciduous teeth are near-universal in this breed and periodontal disease follows them. By three or four years many Yorkies have a mouth that hurts. The owner sees a dog pick up a kibble, drop it, and walk away, then adds warm water or a topper. Softened food is easier to eat and the dog eats more of it, so weight climbs while the mouth gets worse.

Weighing a Six-Pound Dog

Step on the bathroom scale holding the dog and you get a number that is off by a pound in either direction. On a six-pound dog that is 17 percent, larger than the entire weight-loss target. Use a kitchen scale with a plastic tub and tare it, or the clinic's scale once a month.

Body condition beats the number anyway, and on this breed it is the only honest measure. The coat hangs straight off the ribs and hides the waist completely. Put your hands on the dog. A rib should feel like the back of your hand with a thin glove on. The spine should be findable without being sharp. If you have to press to find a rib, the dog is over its ceiling no matter what the scale said.

Three findings move a Yorkie out of the calculator and into an exam room. Weight coming off without a reduced ration. A cough that sounds like a goose when you lift the dog. A dog that picks up a kibble and drops it. Each one has a test attached, and none of them is solved by changing the food.

The rest is a kitchen scale and a hard stop at seven pounds that the breed standard has carried for well over a century.
